Abstract
As floating offshore wind (FOSW) becomes a growing part of California’s and the fFederal gGovernment’s renewable energy plans, addressing potential risks and environmental impacts is increasingly important. Large-scale FOSW projects introduce infrastructure into regions that have historically remained undeveloped. The Mooring Line Sensors for Environmental Awareness (MoorSEA) initiative is focused on developing new technologies to detect primary and secondary entanglements, as well as collisions with mooring lines. This system is designed to work together with human operators, providing real-time alerts when entanglements occur on the mooring lines of floating offshore wind turbines.
Although FOSW mooring lines are large, thick, and under high tension, they are not inherently considered a major entanglement risk. However, abandoned fishing gear, nets, or lines that become snagged on a mooring line or are already ensnaring marine animals can create dangerous secondary entanglement hazards. Determining the capabilities and constraints of an entanglement detection system is essential for shaping future research and refining environmental monitoring strategies. Advancing these technologies will contribute to reducing secondary entanglement risks for marine mammals and other large marine species along the West Coast and in other areas where FOSW infrastructure is deployed.
This Technical Design Report (TDR) presents the engineering design of the MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package. It consolidates the system architecture, subsystem definitions, and design decisions that emerged from the requirements established in the earlier Design Criteria Report (DCR). This document provides the mechanical, electrical, optical, and communication designs for the monitoring package and establishes the configuration of each subsystem as it will exist at the conclusion of the design phase.
The report also defines the interfaces that allow the monitoring package to operate as an auxiliary payload on a variety of host ROV platforms. It describes the system-level structure, sensing capabilities, power and communication pathways, and environmental design considerations that enable consistent and repeatable operation in offshore conditions. The  verification, validation, and field testing of the monitoring package will be addressed separately in the forthcoming Testing and Validation Report. TheseTogether, these documents form the technical foundation for future fabrication, integration, or in-water evaluation of the MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package.

Executive Summary
This Technical Design Report (TDR) presents the engineering design of the MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package, a modular sensing system developed to support visual detection and documentation of secondary entanglements on floating offshore wind mooring lines. Building upon the performance objectives defined in the Design Criteria Report (DCR), this document consolidates the mechanical, electrical, optical, and communication designs that constitute the completed system baseline at the conclusion of the design phase.
The monitoring package integrates a coordinated suite of subsea sensing capabilities, including high-definition visual imaging, stereo depth perception, adaptive lighting, sonar imaging, navigation and positioning support, communication pathways, and a pressure capable mechanical structure. Each subsystem is designed to operate in offshore conditions while maintaining compatibility with a range of host ROV platforms through a defined set of mechanical and electrical interfaces. 
Together, these subsystems are designed to meet the performance goals established in the DCR. The visual sensing architecture provides high-quality video suitable for both real-time operator review and possible future automated analysis. The supporting subsystems supply the navigation context and environmental awareness required for consistent and repeatable mooring-line inspections.
Several design tradeoffs shaped the final configuration. The project favored modular housings over shared enclosures to maximize maintainability, adopted common penetrator and whip conventions for cross-platform compatibility, and selected sensing and lighting components that balance optical performance with power and bandwidth constraints. The design also reflects an emphasis on serviceability, with replaceable modules and a clearly structured system layout.
This Technical Design Report (TDR) establishes the baseline for the monitoring package. It serves as the authoritative reference for the system as defined at the end of the design phase and provides the foundation upon which any subsequent activities may build.

1.3 System Overview
The MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package is a sensing platform designed to assist an ROV operator in documenting and assessing the condition of floating offshore wind mooring lines. It collects high-quality visual, positional, and navigation-related data along the mooring line, enabling operators and future automated tools to identify secondary entanglements and other anomalies during inspection missions.
At a conceptual level, the system combines several major capability areas: visual sensing, lighting, sonar, navigation and positioning support, communications and power distribution. Together, these elements provide a coordinated sensing suite that supports both real-time inspection and post-mission review.
The monitoring package is deployed as an auxiliary payload on a host ROV. The ROV supplies mobility, primary piloting control, communications and electrical power, and it must implement the defined mechanical, electrical, and communication interfaces required for full compatibility with the monitoring package. Through this integration, the monitoring package functions as a specialized, self-contained inspection tool that enhances the host ROV’s ability to conduct consistent and repeatable mooring line surveys.
1.4 Design Philosophy and Guiding Principles
The design of the MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package follows a set of guiding principles intended to ensure technical robustness and practical field usability. These principles emphasize modularity and maintainability so that the system can be integrated with a range of host ROV platforms without major modification. Each subsystem is conceived as a replaceable, pressure-capable unit that supports straightforward service actions.
Reliability under subsea conditions is a central consideration. The system is designed with the intent to tolerate offshore pressures, variable temperatures, and challenging visibility conditions while avoiding unnecessary disturbance to the surrounding marine environment. The overall philosophy also reflects the need for safe handling, repeatable operation, and clear delineation between subsea and topside responsibilities.
1.5 Development Methodology
The development of the monitoring package follows a requirements-driven engineering process grounded in the performance objectives defined in the DCR. Each subsystem is developed through an iterative approach in which concepts are reviewed, refined, and aligned with their respective requirements before being incorporated into the overall architecture.
This workflow ensures that design decisions remain traceable to their originating requirements. Subsystems are developed in parallel, with integration occurring only after their functional roles and constraints are well established.

Chapter 2: System Architecture
This chapter defines the overall architecture of the MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package, describing how power, data, and control are distributed between the Command and Control Bottle (CCB) and a network of external modules including visual, lighting, sonar, and navigation components. It establishes how these subsystems interconnect through shared power and Ethernet-based communication infrastructure to operate as a unified system. The architecture reflects the design philosophy established in the DCR.
Section 2.1 introduces the functional system architecture, showing the overall power, data, and control flow between the monitoring package and the host ROV. Section 2.2 details the hardware architecture, including subsystem partitioning, pressure vessels, and external connections.
2.1 Functional Architecture Overview
The functional architecture defines how power, data, and control flow through the MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package and its interface with the host ROV. This view illustrates the logical relationships among all major subsystems, including visual sensing, lighting, sonar, navigation and positioning, and communication, showing how they operate together as a coordinated system. The architecture also establishes how the monitoring package connects electrically and logically to the host ROV, receiving power and network connectivity while returning real-time sensor data for topside display and recording.
The system is organized around a CCB, which acts as the hub for power distribution and network communication. The host ROV provides either a 24-48 VDC supply or a 120-240 VAC supply and a high-bandwidth fiber Ethernet link to the monitoring package CCB. Internal converters regulate voltages (for example, 24 V and 12 V rails) for individual subsystems. All external modules, including the camera assemblies, lighting units, sonar, and navigation sensors, connect to the CCB through dedicated subsea cable assemblies carrying both power and Ethernet data.
Within the CCB, an Ethernet switch serves as the central network hub, linking all subsystem data streams to the host ROV’s fiber network. The switch provides internal routing between the connected sensors and a single uplink to the host ROV’s provided Ethernet connection, ensuring that the subsea subsystems and the topside command and control computer operate on the same logical network. All data aggregation, logging, and user interaction occurs topside. Command and configuration signals travel from the topside system to the subsea subsystems over the same network path, enabling real-time coordination and control across the package.

At a system level, the monitoring package functions as an integrated extension of the host ROV, drawing both power and communication through a shared umbilical. Within the CCB, internal converters regulate the primary voltage into secondary rails that feed each external subsystem through dedicated subsea connectors. A network switch links all modules including cameras, lights, sonar, and navigation sensors onto a common Ethernet subnet.
Control and telemetry data are exchanged using lightweight, connectionless protocols such as UDP, used for low-latency telemetry and control, allowing real-time operation with minimal overhead. All configuration and coordination functions are managed by the topside command and control computer, which supervises subsystem health and synchronizes timing. The architecture maintains a strict separation between power distribution and data transport, ensuring signal integrity and simplifying fault isolation.
This design supports a modular integration strategy: each subsystem connects through standardized electrical and data interfaces, enabling replacement, upgrades, or expansion without modifying the CCB or host interface. The result is a flexible platform architecture that can adapt to future mission requirements and Hhost-ROV variations with minimal redesign.

2.2 Hardware Architecture
2.2.1 Overview and Physical Partitioning
The hardware architecture defines the physical realization of the MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package, translating the functional relationships shown in Figure 2-1 into distinct enclosures, interconnections, and mounting structures. The system is organized around a Command and Control Bottle (CCB), which houses the power-conversion electronics, network switch, and supervisory microcontroller. This bottle serves as the core of the monitoring package, providing regulated power and Ethernet connectivity to all external modules. External subsystems including cameras, lighting units, sonar, navigation sensors, and any auxiliary instruments connect through short, modular whips terminated in standardized subsea connectors. Each whip carries both power and data to its associated module, minimizing penetrations through the main housing while maintaining serviceability.
Physically, the monitoring package follows a distributed architecture:
Internal components (power supplies, controllers, and communication hardware) are isolated within dry, pressure-rated enclosures for protection and ease of maintenance.
External modules are individually sealed and mechanically mounted to the monitoring-package frame, positioned for optimal field of view and illumination.
Interconnecting whips provide flexible routing between modules and the CCBmain vessel, using subsea-rated cables and strain-relief fittings to prevent mechanical stress at connector interfaces.


This partitioning approach provides environmental protection for internal electronics while maintaining modularity through externally mounted, quick-disconnect sensor modules. Grouping power and network hardware within a single vessel simplifies internal wiring and improves shielding, while distributing sensors externally allows flexible placement and easy subsystem replacement. Connector genders, pinouts, and signal assignments for each penetrator are defined in Appendix B.
The overall mechanical arrangement allows the monitoring package to be mounted as a self-contained unit on a variety of host ROVs. 
2.2.2 Command and Control Bottle (CCB) 
2.2.2.1 Purpose and Function
The CCB serves as the protective housing for all internal components of the MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package. It provides a dry, pressure capable environment that allows reliable operation of critical electronics during deep-water deployment while maintaining accessibility for inspection and service.
The CCB alsois vessel functions as the power and data hub for the entire system. It receives electrical power and network connectivity from the Hhost ROV, converts and distributes regulated voltages to downstream subsystems, and routes high-speed Ethernet traffic between the connected sensors and the topside control computer. Internally, the CCBvessel contains both high- and low-voltage electronics arranged in separate sections to minimize electromagnetic interference.
In addition to the main control and power systems, the CCBvessel houses the bridge  microcontroller, which interfaces with all non-Ethernet based sensors. This controller translates serial or analog data into Ethernet packets for routing across the common network, ensuring that all subsystem data are accessible through a unified communication layer.
External interfaces are provided through a set of well-defined hull penetrators that carry power, data, and control signals to and from external modules. 
The CCB is designed to integrate mechanically within the monitoring package’s mounting system, providing structural attachment points for installation on the host ROV while maintaining serviceability as a self-contained module.
2.2.2.2 External Interfaces and Hull Penetrators
All electrical and optical connections between the internal electronics and the external subsystems are routed through a set of sealed hull penetrators installed on Endcap 1 and Endcap 2 of the CCB. These penetrators provide the transition points between the pressure-capable internal assembly and the distributed modules of the monitoring package.
A pair of penetrators on Endcap 1 supports the combined host whip that links the monitoring package to the ROV. One carries the incoming electrical power and the other carries the fiber-optic data connection. Within the whip, these paths are bundled together for handling convenience but remain electrically and mechanically independent. The remaining penetrators serve internal system interconnections, linking cameras, lights, sonar, navigation sensors, and auxiliary modules to the internal network and regulated power rails.
For all power-carrying interfaces, male connectors are used on power-input penetrators to ensure that energized contacts are recessed within the housing, while female connectors are used on power-output penetrators to prevent exposure of live pins.

Regions follow the expected cable ingress and module locations and include: power conversion and distribution,  processing and communications, and the sensor and Ethernet switch area. Keeping these regions physically separated reduces coupling between power and signal domains, shortens high current loops, and simplifies harness routing during integration and troubleshooting.
The mechanical mounting and thermal strategy are shown in Figure 2-2-2B and C. A webbed base plate attaches to the end cap and supports a longitudinal rectangular tube that runs along the length of the enclosure. The PCBs mount to this structural spine, and the higher dissipation components are mechanically coupled to it. This creates a highly conductive thermal path from heat generating components to the surrounding seawater via the PCB copper, mounting hardware, square tube, base plate, and end cap. . Placing the power conversion hardware closest to the end cap improves heat transfer and reduces internal temperature rise.
The square tube also provides an electromagnetic compatibility boundary, consistent with the internal arrangement shown in Figure 2-2-2C. It acts as a conductive barrier that can be used to shield sensitive signals from the power conversion region and other noise sources. Where practical, low level and timing sensitive wiring is routed on the protected side of the tube and carried toward the bulkheads with reduced exposure to radiated fields. Internal cable routing maintains separation between power and signal harnesses, with strain relief at termination points to protect penetrators and connectors.
Figure 2-2-2B and D show the axial sequencing of the internal modules. The power stage is placed nearest the end cap and mounting plate, followed by the control node that houses the microcontroller and core control electronics. The navigation and ethernet switch stage, including the NaviGuider and the Botblox switch assembly, is placed above the control node. The highest dissipation and highest noise functions are placed nearest the strongest thermal sink and the most controlled return paths. The control and navigation electronics are physically separated and therefore easier to protect from interference.
2.2.2.4 Environmental Integrity and Safety Considerations
This section outlines how the CCB preserves its internal environment and ensures safe operation of all subsystems. It covers sealing, thermal management, electrical protection, electromagnetic compatibility, corrosion control, and operational safety. These measures maintain reliability in subsea conditions and prevent electrical or handling hazards during operation and service. 
2.2.2.4-A Sealing and Pressure Integrity
The CCB maintains a watertight, pressure-capable environment that protects all internal electronics from seawater intrusion and pressure effects. The sealing method ensures long-term integrity of the enclosure while supporting repeated access for maintenance without compromising reliability.
Each endcap incorporates a dual O-ring seal arrangement that provides two independent barriers against leakage. The O-rings are seated in precision-machined grooves and compressed axially when the endcaps are secured to the housing and a vacuum is applied through the port plug. The penetrators installed in each endcap use their own dedicated sealing systems, separate from the endcap-to-housing interface, so that the main vessel seal and the connector seals remain functionally independent. 
A dedicated vacuum or pressure-test port (P-14) enables integrity checks prior to deployment. This feature allows the vessel to be evacuated or pressurized slightly to confirm that seals are properly seated and that the enclosure holds pressure over time.

2.2.2.4-B Thermal Management

The CCB is designed to maintain acceptable internal temperatures during continuous operation and to avoid localized overheating of heat generating components. Primary heat sources include the power conversion, distribution components, and supporting control electronics mounted within the enclosure. The thermal design approach is to provide a direct, conductive heat path from these components to the endcaps and then to the surrounding seawater, rather than relying on internal air convection alone.

2.2.2.4-C Electrical Protection, Grounding, and Electromagnetic Compatibility
The electrical system is designed to ensure safe power distribution within the CCB. The selected power supply provides integrated over-current, over-voltage, and short-circuit protection, reducing the need for separate fusing of each subsystem. Surge suppression and inrush-current limiting are included in accordance with DCR §2.6 to prevent startup faults such as breaker trips or voltage sag. Polarity control is maintained through keyed connectors. Power-connector gender logic follows the standard convention: male penetrators are used at power input interfaces, and female connectors are used at power output interfaces so that energized pins are not exposed when unmated.
All internal electronic devices share a common electrical ground through the wiring harness. This ground is bonded to the internal metal mounting plate, forming a single grounding plane for the CCBvessel. The common ground may be tied to the host ROV frame ground at a bonding point to control reference potentials and minimize the risk of ground loops.
Electromagnetic compatibility is maintained through careful internal wiring practices. Differential copper Ethernet lines between the internal switch and the external module whips are twisted to preserve signal balance and reduce electromagnetic noise. Once the signals transition to the short external whips, non-differential pair wiring is acceptable because of the limited length. Internal wire routing separates power and signal paths to minimize interference, and the compass is mounted as far as practical from high-current wiring and power components to reduce magnetic disturbance.
2.2.2.4-D Corrosion and Environmental Materials Compatibility
The CCB and all external fittings are selected as an integrated assembly designed for long-term subsea exposure. The chosen housing material and its fasteners provide inherent corrosion resistance and galvanic compatibility, minimizing the need for additional isolation hardware. Any secondary components or mounts attached to the CCB vessel will use materials confirmed to be compatible with the enclosure alloy and the host ROV frame. 
2.2.2.4-E Operational Safety
The CCBvessel and its connections are arranged to minimize electrical and mechanical hazards during handling and operation. Power connectors follow recessed-pin and gender conventions that prevent accidental contact with live conductors. Cable whips are routed and strain-relieved to avoid abrasion, snagging, or tension on penetrators. Service ports are clearly identified and intended for use only during controlled maintenance.
2.2.2.5 Design Rationale
The design reflects the modular and maintainable approach established in the DCR.
Collocating the power and control electronics within a bottle minimizes cable length, reducing both electromagnetic interference and signal loss. A single housing also simplifies sealing, since all critical electronics can be accessed and maintained through one enclosure. The internal mounting plate provides organized attachment points and straightforward removal for maintenance or upgrades.
External devices such as cameras, lights, sonar, and navigation sensors connect through short modular whips rather than permanent internal wiring. This approach simplifies integration and allows the system to be reconfigured or expanded for future mission profiles without redesigning the core vessel.

3.1 Mounting and ROV Integration
3.1.1 Purpose and integration challenge
The MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package is designed to function as an auxiliary payload across a range of Hhost ROV platforms. That goal creates a core integration challenge: ROV frames, skids, buoyancy layouts, and frontal profiles vary widely, yet the monitoring package must remain hydrodynamically reasonable, mechanically stable, serviceable, and repeatable in its sensor geometry once installed.
This section describes a mounting strategy that supports cross platform use without turning every new vehicle integration into a full redesign. The approach combines two complementary ideas:
A standardized mechanical interface that defines how the monitoring package attaches to a host ROV, allowing the host vehicle to implement only a vehicle specific docking bracket while the monitoring package side remains consistent.
A generative, constraint driven structural framework that ties together the Command and Control Bottle (CCB) and external modules as a single integrated assembly, while allowing the boundary conditions and clearance envelopes to be adapted to the geometry of each Hhost ROV.
The monitoring package remains a coherent payload with stable internal sensor relationships, while the interface to each Hhost ROV is implemented through a controlled and repeatable mechanical coupling using  a vehicle specific adapter.
3.1.2 Monitoring package mounting framework concept
Modern CAD workflows make it practical to treat packaging, clearances, and structural integration as a single, model-driven design problem. In this project, the mounting framework is developed using a constraint-based CAD approach in which the CCB and external modules are placed first, required sightlines and keep-clear zones are established, and the supporting structure is then shaped around those constraints to suit the host vehicle. This enables a repeatable integration process while still allowing the framework geometry to be adapted for different host ROV frames.
The Monitoring Package mounting framework positions and supports the external modules so that sightlines and illumination geometry remain controlled and repeatable. The framework is implemented as a custom 3D-printed structure that provides defined attachment points for each external module, maintaining stable orientation and separation while preserving camera sightlines and minimizing backscatter from nearby illumination. The framework also incorporates serviceability and handling features, including access to connectors and fasteners and lift or grasp points that support safe handling and maintenance without disturbing the primary seals of the central pressure vessel. Details on how this is implemented in CAD are covered in section 3.1.3. 
The structural design is then driven by explicit load cases and constraints that represent real handling and subsea operation. These inputs include lifting and deck handling loads during installation and removal, steady hydrodynamic forces and vibration during operation, minor impacts from incidental contact during inspection, and cable loads that ensure penetrators and connectors are not forced to act as strain relief. Interface loads transmitted through the host adapter are also considered so that alignment remains stable and the payload does not shift under operational forces. In this approach, the loads and constraints function as the primary design definition, turning a vehicle-specific packaging problem into a repeatable engineering process.Vehicle-specific hydrodynamics are addressed by applying keep-clear volumes for thruster flow paths and moving mechanisms and by constraining the framework to remain within a packaging envelope that discourages protrusions and minimizes flow-disrupting surfaces. This strategy allows the same monitoring package to be positioned in the most hydrodynamically favorable region on each host ROV while preserving the controlled sensor geometry.
The baseline framework is designed for additive manufacturing so that cable guides, curved ribs, and compound interfaces can be integrated into a single structure rather than assembled from many small parts. The resulting design is evaluated for manufacturability, including print orientation and support strategy, integration of embedded features such as channels, tie points, and alignment surfaces, compatibility with the fasteners and inserts used to mount modules and attach to the host bracket, and practical post-processing steps appropriate for subsea exposure.

3.3 Implementation for Testing
A baseline ROV frame equipped with a tilt mechanism was selected as the primary integration test case for the monitoring package. This frame provides a representative geometry, known mounting members, and a realistic set of constraints for clearances, cable routing, and service access. It also allows repeatable fit checks and rapid iteration without the added complexity of vehicle-specific payloads or mission tooling.
While the monitoring package is intended to be deployed across multiple existing ROVs, the initial design and validation effort intentionally uses a simplified, blank host configuration. Starting from a clean reference frame makes it easier to control variables, document interfaces, and establish a robust mounting approach before adapting to the unique layouts of other vehicles. Once the baseline integration is proven, only the minimal interface geometry required to attach to a given host frame will be adjusted, while the monitoring package and its alignment-critical features remain consistent.
Host ROVs vary in whether they include a dedicated tilt mechanism, and this has a direct impact on how sensors should be mounted. To support broad compatibility, the monitoring package is designed as a modular system that can be configured as fully static, or a hybrid arrangement depending on the host vehicle and mission needs.
A fully static configuration is appropriate for platforms without a tilt axis, or where simplicity and mechanical robustness are prioritized. In this mode, components are mounted to fixed frame members, and aiming or coverage is achieved through sensor selection, placement, and field of view rather than mechanical motion.
For platforms equipped with a tilt mechanism, alignment-critical sensors that benefit from controlled pointing can be mounted on the tiltable stage. This allows the vehicle to adjust viewing angle without reorienting the entire ROV, improving targeting of features of interest and enabling more consistent imaging or inspection angles.
For the primary integration test case, a hybrid approach was selected that separates components into static, frame-mounted hardware and tilt-mounted, alignment-critical sensors. The lights, USBL transponder, and auxiliary electronics (CCB) are mounted statically to the host frame (Figure 3-1-4A, C). Keeping these items on the fixed structure minimizes moving mass on the tilt axis, reduces dynamic cable motion and bend cycling, and simplifies installation across different vehicles by using a small set of clamp-on interface parts. This strategy  also helps preserve vehicle balance and reduces the likelihood that tilt motion is limited by bulky peripherals or wiring.
The sonar, stereo camera assembly, and Main Camera are mounted on the tilt mechanism (Figure 3-1-3D) to take full advantage of controlled pointing. Grouping these sensors on the same moving stage maintains coordinated aim and consistent relative alignment between modalities, which improves interpretation of imagery and acoustic returns during operations. It also allows operators to adjust the view angle without repositioning the entire ROV, supporting both inspection-style tasks and target reacquisition in cluttered environments. Overall, this split preserves a controlled “core” payload assembly (Figure 3-1-3B) while limiting vehicle-specific changes to the minimum static bracket geometry (Figure 3-1-3C), balancing compatibility, mechanical simplicity, and operational flexibility.

3.3 Thermal and Environmental Design
3.3.1 Overview
3.3.1 Overview
The MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package is intended for repeated deployment in harsh marine environments where pressure, temperature variation, corrosion, biofouling, and handling loads can degrade performance over time. The thermal and environmental design approach focuses on maintaining reliable operation of all electronics and sensors across expected deployment conditions, protecting components from seawater exposure and pressure cycling, and preserving long-term serviceability through materials choices, sealing strategy, and maintainable assembly practices. This section summarizes the primary environmental threats and the design strategies used to mitigate them.
3.3.2 Environmental conditions and primary risks
The monitoring package must function in conditions that are inherently hostile to typical electronics and mechanical assemblies. Key risks include hydrostatic pressure and pressure cycling during repeated dives, seawater exposure from salt ingress, splash, and trapped moisture, temperature variation during storage, deck handling, and in-water operation, corrosion including galvanic interactions between dissimilar metals, biofouling that can reduce optical clarity and sensor performance, and mechanical shock and vibration during launch, recovery, and transport. The overall design philosophy assumes that seawater will contact external surfaces, pressure cycles will occur regularly, and maintenance access will be required. The design therefore emphasizes robust sealing, corrosion control, and modular service.
3.3.3 Pressure and seawater protection strategy
Core electronics are housed inside a dedicated pressure vessel to isolate sensitive components from direct seawater exposure and hydrostatic pressure. External sensors and modules are selected and packaged for subsea use, or are protected by dedicated housings appropriate to their exposure and depth rating. Pressure and seawater protection is achieved through a primary pressure boundary implemented with proven sealing interfaces, penetrators and connectors selected to maintain pressure integrity while still supporting field service and modular replacement, and internal layout practices that reduce the risk of condensation-related failures through controlled cable routing, strain relief, and limiting exposure of high-impedance nodes where practical. Where feasible, the design avoids relying on potting as the primary protection method for serviceable electronics. Potting may be used selectively for strain relief or localized hardening, but the preferred approach is to protect electronics through pressure-rated housings and connectorized interfaces that support repair and replacement.
3.3.4 Corrosion control and material compatibility
The marine environment creates aggressive corrosion conditions, particularly when dissimilar metals are electrically coupled in seawater. Corrosion is addressed through material selection and mechanical detailing. External hardware and structures prioritize corrosion-resistant alloys and plastics. Dissimilar metal couples are minimized where possible and isolated where unavoidable using nonconductive bushings, coatings, or gasket interfaces. Fasteners and inserts are selected for marine exposure with consideration for both corrosion resistance and galling risk, and geometry is designed to avoid water traps by providing drainage paths and surfaces that do not retain seawater after recovery. Where externally exposed metallic components are required, protective coatings and controlled isolation strategies are applied to reduce galvanic risk while keeping assemblies maintainable.
3.3.5 Thermal design approach
ermal performance is driven by internal electronics dissipation, heat transfer through the pressure vessel, and the surrounding seawater environment. During submerged operation, seawater provides an effective heat sink and supports heat rejection through conductive paths from internal electronics to the vessel wall. Worst-case temperatures may occur during deck handling, pre-dive staging, or post-dive recovery when the system is powered but not submerged, particularly in warm ambient air or under solar loading. Electronics are arranged to avoid localized hot spots, with higher dissipation devices placed to promote conduction into the housing and reduce thermal gradients across sensitive components.
Thermal management is addressed primarily through passive means. Higher power components are conductively coupled to the pressure vessel or internal heat spreaders, and component placement is used to support internal airflow where available while remaining robust to vibration and handling. Temperature-sensitive parts such as voltage regulators, power conversion stages, and any internal illumination drivers are operated with conservative margins. Where external modules generate heat, such as high-output lights, their housing design and mounting are intended to transfer heat into surrounding water and avoid trapping heat within enclosed cavities.


Chapter 4: Electrical and Power System Design
4.1 Power Distribution Overview
The monitoring package receives power from the host ROV through a subsea-capable wet-mate connector. Primary operation is from a nominal 24 V to 48 VDC supply. To support host platforms that deliver AC input power, the design also accommodates an optional 120 VAC to 240 VAC input stage. In the AC-input configuration, incoming power is fused and filtered, then rectified to a high-voltage DC bus before a dedicated AC-DC stage generates an isolated, regulated 24 VDC system bus. This 24 V bus then feeds the same downstream distribution and point-of-load regulation used in the DC-input configuration.

Input power is routed to a central distribution board that provides protected branch circuits and rail monitoring. High-power loads are supplied from the 24 V rail, while intermediate loads may be served from a regulated 12 V rail. Local point-of-load regulators generate 5 V and 3.3 V as required for compute, communications, and sensors. Where practical, rail voltage, current, and fault status are measured on the distribution board and reported to the surface to support health monitoring and troubleshooting.

Where the host ROV explicitly supports higher available power, an optional host-configurable “high power” mode can raise or remove the LED cap to take advantage of the additional supply capacity, while still preserving soft-start behavior, monitoring, and protections to prevent abrupt transients or uncontrolled overdraw.

4.4 Protection and Fault Tolerance
The power system is designed to protect the monitoring package and the individual components connect to it. Branch circuits from the distribution/backplane board include overcurrent protection sized to prevent wiring damage and to isolate faults to the smallest practical subsystem. Electrical isolation measures are included to prevent package faults from propagating into the host ROV power system.
Inrush limiting is implemented through soft-start and staged activation. High-demand loads are soft started to reduce peak startup current. Where practical, the system reports rail voltages and current draw to the surface to enable early detection of anomalous behavior.
Failsafe behavior is implemented such that a power interruption or loss of communication results in a safe state (shutdown or standby), without imposing risk to the host ROV or creating uncontrolled loads.
4.5 Signal Integrity and Noise Management
Given high-current switching regulators, power delivery networks  and sensitive analog sensors in a compact enclosure, EMI is treated as a primary design constraint. The signal integrity plan includes: shielded cabling for high-speed or noise-sensitive signals, deliberate routing/segregation of noisy power switching elements away from sensitive analog circuits, and grounding/return-path control to reduce conducted and radiated emissions.
Digital and analog ground domains are separated where needed, with controlled single-point or defined-impedance connections to manage return currents. Filtering elements such as ferrite beads, common-mode chokes, and local decoupling capacitance are applied at module boundaries and at cable ingress points. Where warranted, shielding compartments or physical separation are used to reduce crosstalk and protect critical instrumentation interfaces.

5.1 Visual Sensing Subsystem
5.1.1 Purpose and Scope
The visual sensing subsystem serves as the primary observation component of the MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package. It provides high quality video data that supports both real-time human-in-the-loop inspection and future automated detection of secondary entanglements on offshore wind mooring lines. It is designed to capture fine details such as fishing line, net fragments, and biological growth while maintaining wide contextual coverage of mooring structures and surrounding habitat.
The subsystem consists of one stereo camera pair (two matched modules) and one Main Camera, each housed in an independent pressure vessel with dedicated subsea cable connections to the Command and Control Bottle (CCB), and a supplementary low-latency camera. The stereo cameras are configured to supply timestamp synchronized image pairs optimized for short-range (1–5 m) depth perception, while the Main Camera and low-latency camera provide a video feeds for operator navigation and situational awareness, enabling the detection of multiple or small potential entanglements within the  field of view.
All cameras share the same power distribution and data network, originating from the CCB, and are connected through a common Ethernet switch. During recording, each video stream is timestamped to support correlation across sources. Together, these components form the core visual foundation for both manual and future automated entanglement analysis within the MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package.
The design and performance objectives for this subsystem are defined in DCR §2.1 – Visual Sensing Requirements, which establishes the baseline criteria for resolution, frame rate, field of view, low-light performance, and environmental tolerance.

5.1.4 Stereo Camera Assembly
5.1.4.1 Purpose and Functional Role
The stereo camera system provides the Monitoring Package with depth perception and 3D scene understanding within the critical one to five meter inspection range identified in DCR §2.1. By capturing synchronized left and right images across a fixed width baseline, the system enables disparity-based depth estimation, enhanced operator awareness, and the foundation for future machine learning analysis. 
To complement this goal of disparity-based depth estimation, the stereo camera assembly includes 2 lasers to assist with depth estimation, a forward range altimeter, and an additional low-latency camera. Integrating data from these sensors provides more accuracy to our stereo camera’s depth perception. The added forward range altimeter will provide supplemental range data. Parallel lasers spaced at 10 cm provide an on-screen scale reference that supports standoff estimation and approximate sizing in recorded imagery.
During a typical monitoring pass described in the Concept of Operations (DCR §3), the stereo system and forward altimeter helps the operator maintain safe standoff distance, interpret the geometry of chains, synthetic lines, and accessory hardware, and detect secondary wraps or protruding materials that may not be easily interpreted in monocular imagery. The system's depth cues supplement the Main Camera feed while the Main Camera and low-latency camera provides high fidelity context for navigation and anomaly recognition. The stereo system resolves local spatial relationships that are usefulessential for assessing possible entanglements and for supporting later automated analysis.
The stereo system therefore fills a specific functional role within the visual sensing subsystem by improving spatial awareness in low visibility subsea environments, supportsing real time human in the loop inspection, and enablesenabling the computational pathways required for future machine learning driven depth mapping and geometric reconstruction.
5.1.4.2 Trace to Design Criteria Report
The stereo camera system fulfills the depth-related visual sensing requirements defined in Table 5.1 and derived from DCR §2.1. It primarily satisfies VS-5 (Stereoscopic Imaging) by providing a fixed baseline and matched camera units that deliver depth perception within the required one to five meter operating range. The system also supports VS-3 (Field of View) through lens and placement choices that maintain sufficient overlap for stereo matching, and VS-6 (Camera Placement) by complementing the Main Camera with dedicated depth-aware coverage along the primary inspection path described in the Concept of Operations from both the stereo camera system and the additional low-latency camera. Requirements related to resolution, frame rate, low-light performance, and maintainability (VS-1, VS-2, VS-4, and VS-8) are met through the selection of high-resolution cameras integrated into the appropriate housings.
Together, these design elements ensure that the stereo subsystem aligns with the intent of the Design Criteria Report by providing depth-aware imagery that supports both real-time interpretation and the future integration of machine-assisted 3D reconstruction and analysis tools.
5.1.4.3 Stereo Module Architecture and Identical Unit Concept
The stereo system consists of two identical camera modules mounted in a fixed geometric arrangement to form a single stereo assembly. Each module is enclosed, and the two housings are linked by a rigid mechanical connection that establishes a fixed 200 mm baseline. This rigid assembly is then anchored to the rest of the mount holding the lasers, forward altimeter, and low-latency camera. This 200mm separation is selected to support reliable depth reconstruction in the one to five meter range defined in the Design Criteria Report.
Both camera modules use identical sensors, optics, mounts, and electrical interfaces. Matching the modules simplifies calibration, ensures consistent intrinsic parameters between the left and right views, and maintains stable stereo geometry across deployments. The mechanical connection between the housings preserves the relative orientation of the optical axes, preventing drift or flexure that could degrade disparity accuracy.
The stereo assembly mounts to the Monitoring Package frame as described in Chapter 2, maintaining a consistent orientation relative to the ROV’s forward inspection corridor. This integrated configuration provides a repeatable alignment reference, minimizes recalibration needs after servicing, and establishes the depth-aware viewing region used for mooring-line inspection.
5.1.4.4 Placement and Orientation on the Monitoring Package
The stereo assembly is mounted at the forward center of the Monitoring Package frame, aligned with the ROV’s primary inspection corridor. Its optical axes point directly ahead to support depth perception and disparity accuracy during the level-flight monitoring passes defined in the Concept of Operations. This placement ensures that the stereo system observes the same operational volume in which the Main Camera provides broader situational imagery, allowing the two systems to deliver complementary, non-overlapping capabilities.
A dedicated fixed mounting bracket secures the stereo assembly to the frame. The bracket incorporates fixed mechanical reference surfaces that maintain the 200 mm baseline and the relative orientation of the two camera modules across deployments. The assembly is not adjustable in operation, ensuring that stereo geometry remains stable as long as the complete assembly remains intact. Individual camera modules may be detached and replaced for service, but doing so alters the mechanical relationship between views and therefore requires a full stereo recalibration before redeployment.
Positioning the stereo assembly at the forward center of the frame provides an unobstructed viewing path and aligns the depth-aware field of view with the operator’s primary region of interest during mooring-line inspection. The placement integrates cleanly with the sensor arrangement defined in Chapter 2, maintaining consistent spatial relationships between the stereo assembly and the other visual sensing elements.

The stereo assembly is configured to provide reliable depth perception across the intended 1 to 10 meter inspection range defined for mooring line monitoring. The two cameras are mounted with a fixed 200 mm baseline that supports accurate 3D interpretation of the near field inspection corridor and provides continuity of depth cues across the broader 10 meter region used during typical monitoring passes. 5.1.4.6 Lens and Distortion Management
The stereo assembly uses fixed focal length lenses that produce an approximately 85 degree horizontal field of view and 55 degree vertical field of view. Both cameras use identical lenses and identical sensor formats, ensuring matched intrinsic characteristics and simplifying rectification and depth estimation.
Because fixed lenses naturally exhibit geometric distortion, each camera undergoes intrinsic calibration during system integration. This process determines the focal length, principal point, and distortion coefficients needed to linearize incoming imagery. The resulting parameters are stored with the stereo assembly configuration and applied either in real time or during post mission processing to un-distort each frame using a planar homography based correction model derived from the calibration procedure.
Applying calibrated undistortion ensures that both left and right images share a consistent projection geometry, which is essential for accurate rectification and disparity computation.
Additional details on the optical calibration workflow are provided in §5.1.4.12.

5.1.4.8 Mounting to the Monitoring Package Frame
The stereo assembly is secured to the Monitoring Package frame using a fixed mounting component described in Chapter 3. This interface positions the assembly at the required forward-looking orientation and holds it rigidly so that the stereo geometry remains constant across deployments.
The mounting component incorporates alignment features that preserve the 200 millimeter baseline and maintain the parallel orientation of the two optical axes. By constraining the assembly to a single designed orientation with no field adjustable elements, the mount ensures that the stereo configuration remains repeatable following routine handling and installation.
Although the complete assembly is mechanically stable once mounted, replacing either individual camera housing alters the geometric relationship between the left and right views. Any such replacement requires a full stereo recalibration prior to redeployment to restore the extrinsic alignment needed for depth estimation and rectified stereo processing.
5.1.4.9 Power and Communication Interfaces
Each stereo camera receives power from the Monitoring Package’s 12 volt distribution system, with electrical load details provided in §4.2. Power is delivered through the dedicated subsea cables and SubConn MicroCircular 8 pin bulkhead penetrators on each housing. 
Both cameras stream RTSP video over Ethernet. Their network lines enter the CCB at the stereo assembly penetrator and are routed through the internal switch, creating two independent network endpoints for the left and right views.
The power and Ethernet cables from each housing are routed along the rigid connection between the cameras and terminate at a shared strain relief and penetrator location on the Monitoring Package frame.
5.1.4.10 Addressing and Logical Integration
Each camera in the stereo assembly is assigned a unique network identity using either static IP configuration through the camera’s web interface or dynamic assignment when a DHCP service is available on the ROV network. A consistent naming convention, such as STEREO_LEFT and STEREO_RIGHT, ensures that both the operational interface and the recorded metadata clearly identify each camera stream.
Although the two camera modules appear on the network as independent Ethernet devices, the Monitoring Package treats their outputs as coordinated channels of a single stereo sensor. The pair shares a unified calibration solution, a common baseline definition, and a single configuration entry within the system. During data acquisition, the metadata model keeps the streams associated so that disparity estimation and time alignment always treat the left and right images as linked elements of one sensing system.
5.1.4.11 Synchronization and Frame Alignment
Accurate synchronization is essential for stereo imaging because depth estimation requires comparing two views of the scene captured at the same moment. Even small temporal offsets between the left and right frames can degrade disparity accuracy or introduce misleading depth cues.
The stereo system uses a software based synchronization approach built on timestamp alignment. Each camera embeds a one second resolution timestamp in its video stream, and Network Time Protocol (NTP) keeps the two clocks aligned within this resolution. Host side arrival times are then used to sequence the frames within each one second interval.
Within each timestamped interval, the system receives a matching set of frames from the left and right cameras. Frames are paired by index number in that interval, so left frame n is matched with right frame n. Because the stereo assembly maintains a fixed baseline and parallel optical axes, this method provides stable and repeatable alignment across the intended one to ten meter operating range.
This architecture intentionally avoids hardware trigger lines. Hardware triggering would require electrical signaling between housings, increase penetrator pin count, and add mechanical and integration complexity. 
5.1.4.12 Calibration and Configuration Management
Each camera in the stereo assembly requires intrinsic calibration to determine its focal length, principal point, and distortion parameters. The assembly also relies on an extrinsic calibration that defines the fixed geometric relationship between the left and right views. This relationship is essential for depth estimation and must be recalculated whenever either camera housing is removed or replaced.
Calibration results are stored with the stereo assembly’s configuration data so that the correct parameters are applied during processing and preserved across deployments. Calibration is performed during system integration and confirmed during routine maintenance. Normal cleaning does not affect calibration, but any mechanical disturbance that changes camera alignment requires a full recalibration.
The complete calibration procedure is provided in §5.1.8.
5.1.4.13 Environmental and Reliability Considerations
Each camera in the stereo assembly is housed within an independently sealed 2” inner diameter aluminum  BlueRobotics enclosure intended for subsea deployment. The housings provide environmental protection for the camera electronics and optics through sealed interfaces at the viewport and cable penetrator, preventing water ingress during normal operation.
Thermal behavior within each housing is benign. The cameras have low power dissipation, and passive heat transfer through the enclosure walls is sufficient to maintain operating temperatures within acceptable limits. No active cooling or specialized thermal management features are required.
Mechanical reliability is achieved through the rigid mechanical connection that maintains the fixed baseline and parallel optical alignment between the two cameras. This structure preserves stereo geometry during handling, launch and recovery, and routine ROV maneuvering, ensuring that calibration integrity is not degraded by normal operational loads.
External cabling from each housing is routed along the mounting structure and secured at defined strain relief points. This routing minimizes mechanical stress on connectors and reduces the likelihood of cable fatigue or damage during deployment and recovery.
To prevent internal condensation and preserve optical clarity, each housing includes a desiccant pack that absorbs residual moisture within the sealed volume. This measure reduces the risk of condensation forming on the lens or viewport during temperature changes encountered in subsea operation.
5.1.4.14 Maintainability and Field Service
The stereo camera assembly is designed to support straightforward inspection and servicing between missions. Both camera viewports are externally accessible and may be visually inspected and cleaned without removing the stereo assembly from the Monitoring Package. This allows routine optical maintenance to be performed quickly during deck operations.
Electrical connectors and cable strain relief points associated with the stereo cameras are accessible when the Monitoring Package is out of the water. Individual camera housings can be disconnected without disturbing adjacent subsystems, supporting efficient troubleshooting and replacement if required.
Each camera housing may be removed and replaced as a complete unit. This modular replacement approach limits service actions to the affected housing and avoids disassembly of the stereo assembly or the surrounding structure. Because replacing either housing alters the geometric relationship between the left and right views, any such replacement requires a full stereo recalibration prior to redeployment.
A typical service workflow consists of inspecting and cleaning the viewports, disconnecting and removing a housing if replacement is necessary, installing the replacement unit, and performing stereo recalibration before the next mission. This approach balances ease of field service with the geometric stability required for reliable stereo depth estimation.
System wide maintainability considerations for the Monitoring Package are described in §5.1.10.
5.1.4.15 Performance Mapping Linkage
The stereo camera system addresses the depth perception requirements defined in DCR §2.1, with primary traceability to VS-5 (Stereoscopic Imaging). Its baseline geometry and field-of-view overlap define a depth-aware inspection volume suited to mooring line monitoring.
As shown in Figures 5-1-4-1 and 5-1-4-2, stereo performance is characterized by three regions. The near-field region from approximately 1 to 5 meters provides high depth sensitivity for detailed inspection of mooring components and potential entanglements. The mid-field region from approximately 5 to 10 meters maintains usable depth perception during normal monitoring passes. Beyond this range, depth sensitivity decreases, but the system continues to provide qualitative spatial cues and scene context out to approximately 20 meters.
This performance profile satisfies the intent of DCR §2.1 by optimizing depth perception in the critical inspection range while preserving broader spatial awareness. Mapping of the stereo behavior requirements is summarized in §5.1.11.
5.1.4.16 Risks and Design Considerations
The stereo camera system is designed to provide reliable depth perception within the intended inspection volume. As with any stereo imaging system operating in a subsea environment, its performance is influenced by a small number of known design sensitivities. These considerations are inherent to the chosen architecture and are managed through mechanical design, calibration practices, and data handling strategies described elsewhere in this report.
A primary mechanical risk is disturbance of the fixed stereo baseline due to impact or handling. Accurate depth estimation depends on preservation of the 200 millimeter separation and the relative orientation between the two camera housings. This risk is mitigated through a rigid mechanical connection and fixed mounting geometry that resists relative motion during normal operation. A related risk arises if one camera housing is removed and replaced during maintenance. Any change in the relative position or orientation between the two views alters the stereo geometry and invalidates previously established calibration parameters. This risk is managed procedurally by requiring full stereo recalibration whenever either housing is replaced or temporarily removed, as described in §5.1.8.
Because the stereo system relies on timestamp based frame alignment rather than hardware trigger lines, rapid vehicle motion or transient network effects could introduce pairing ambiguity if timestamps are not well aligned. This risk is mitigated through the use of synchronized camera clocks and host side alignment logic that pairs frames based on timestamp consistency and expected frame cadence. 
Environmental conditions can also affect stereo correlation quality. Suspended particulates, turbidity, and glare from artificial lighting may reduce image contrast and feature matchability between views, particularly at longer ranges. This consideration is addressed through coordinated lighting placement and intensity control, which are discussed in §5.2, rather than through changes to the stereo geometry itself.
Finally, internal condensation or viewport fouling could degrade image clarity and reduce disparity matching performance. Each camera housing incorporates passive moisture control measures to limit internal condensation, and external viewports are accessible for inspection and cleaning between missions. These features support continued optical performance during repeated deployments without introducing additional operational complexity.
Collectively, these considerations reflect known sensitivities of stereo imaging systems in subsea environments. The stereo camera design accounts for these factors through fixed geometry, procedural calibration control, and coordinated system level design, ensuring that depth perception performance remains consistent with the objectives defined in DCR §2.1.

The Main Camera Module serves as the primary visual channel for real-time situational awareness and mooring line entanglement identification within the MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package. It provides a continuous, low-latency video feed optimized for identifying entanglements and maintaining spatial context during inspection operations. The module combines a professional digital camera and photographic-grade lens enclosed within a pressure-capable housing, delivering superior optical quality and wide dynamic range while remaining compatible with the unified 24-volt power and Ethernet network shared across all visual subsystems.
5.1.6.1 Purpose and Functional Role
The Main Camera functions as the system’s central observation instrument, supplying the operator with a stabilized, high-resolution video stream for precise maneuvering and inspection tasks. Its output serves as the visual reference for navigation near mooring lines and for identifying potential secondary entanglements or other anomalies in real time.
Unlike the stereo modules, which are optimized for depth perception at short range, the Main Camera emphasizes image fidelity to support continuous monitoring and effective data collection. The recorded video provides the baseline dataset for subsequent analysis and machine-learning model development.
5.1.6.2 Optical Assembly and Lens Configuration
The Main Camera assembly integrates a professional digital camera (Z-Cam E1504) with a fixed photographic lens selected to provide high optical quality and reliable subsea performance. The chosen lens achieves a balanced field of view suitable for both close-range entanglement detection and general situational awareness at standoff distances of approximately 1–5 m.
The camera and lens are enclosed within a pressure-capable aluminum  housing fitted with a precision acrylic dome port. An internal 3D printed mount aligns the optical axis with the housing centerline and positions the lens center point at the geometric center of the dome, minimizing refraction and distortion across the full image frame. This configuration ensures consistent imaging geometry and optical fidelity in both air and water.
Because the lens provides a variable focal length, the system uses a fixed zoom setting established during assembly to maintain a consistent imaging geometry. This fixed position ensures that the resulting field of view remains stable across deployments and preserves the alignment between the lens and the geometric center of the acrylic dome. The zoom position is locked using the lens’s mechanical controls to prevent drift during handling and subsea operation.
The resulting field of view conforms to DCR §2.1 (VS-3), providing horizontal coverage within the specified 70–140 degree range. This satisfies the requirement for broad contextual visibility of mooring structures and surrounding habitat while maintaining sufficient image detail for identifying small entanglement materials.
5.1.6.3 Housing and Mechanical Integration
The Main Camera is installed within a pressure-capable enclosure consisting of a cylindrical aluminum tube mated to two endcaps. The forward endcap incorporates an acrylic dome that serves as the optical window, while the rear endcap houses the mechanical interfaces for the electrical penetrator and the vacuum port. Both endcaps are secured using common radial O-ring seals to maintain internal environmental integrity during subsea operation.

5.1.6.6 Image Processing and Streaming
The Main Camera provides a continuous, low-latency video stream to the Topside Control Computer using an RTSP-based output. The camera encodes video using standard H.264 or H.265 compression, depending on configuration, producing a stable network stream compatible with the unified Ethernet communication architecture of the Monitoring Package. This stream supports real-time operator navigation and inspection tasks, and forms part of the synchronized multisensor dataset that can be used for post-mission review.
The camera supports multiple recording and streaming resolutions. Available modes include 3840 × 2160 at 29.97, 25, 24, and 23.98 frames per second; 2880 × 2880 at the same frame rates; and 1920 × 1080 at 59.97, 50, 29.97, 25, 24, and 23.98 frames per second. Additional DCI 4K modes are also supported. These options allow the camera to operate within the performance range defined in DCR §2.1 for resolution and frame rate while accommodating different mission objectives and bandwidth conditions.
The camera’s onboard image-processing controls allow adjustment to variable subsea lighting environments. ISO may be set manually within the 800 to 25600 range or left in automatic mode. Shutter speed may be configured manually up to 1/8000 second. White balance supports automatic, preset, and manual modes spanning approximately 2300 K to 7500 K. These controls allow the operator to manage exposure and tonal accuracy in the presence of turbidity, backscatter, and changing lighting conditions.
Each video frame is timestamped internally to maintain alignment with the system time used throughout the Monitoring Package. This supports correlation between the Main Camera stream, the stereo camera system , lighting behavior, and navigation data. Streaming latency and bitrate vary with resolution and compression settings, but can be selected to maintain responsive operator feedback while remaining compatible with the available network bandwidth.
Typical RTSP streams consume approximately 6–12 Mbps at 1080p30 and 32–50 Mbps at 4K30 when encoded using H.264, depending on compression settings and scene complexity. With H.265 encoding enabled, bandwidth requirements are reduced to approximately 3–6 Mbps at 1080p30 and 12–25 Mbps at 4K30.
5.1.6.7 Environmental and Operational Considerations
The Main Camera housing is designed to operate reliably within the environmental conditions expected for offshore ROV deployment. The assembly is compatible with the temperature range defined in the Design Criteria Report, from approximately –2 to 35 °C, and the enclosure is built to withstand external hydrostatic pressure consistent with the Monitoring Package’s intended operating depth. The aluminum tube and endcaps provide structural rigidity under external load, while the acrylic dome maintains optical clarity under the same conditions.
Prior to deployment, the enclosure is verified using the integrated vacuum port to confirm seal integrity. Pulling a vacuum and maintaining it for a defined observation period provides an effective pre-mission check for leaks without requiring external pressure testing equipment. This procedure is performed each time the housing is opened for maintenance or lens adjustment.
Optical performance depends on the external condition of the acrylic dome. The dome should be cleaned with non-abrasive materials prior to deployment to remove any residues, fingerprints, or particulate matter that could scatter light or introduce glare. If required, a hydrophobic coating may be applied to the outer surface of the acrylic to reduce water adhesion and support consistent image clarity during operation. Extended deployments may result in minor biofouling on the acrylic dome; this is mitigated through pre-deployment cleaning procedures and routine maintenance between missions.
Moisture control inside the housing is essential for preventing condensation on the interior surface of the dome or on the camera lens. Heat generated by the power supply and camera can elevate the internal temperature relative to the surrounding environment, creating the potential for condensation if humidity is present. To prevent this, a fresh desiccant pack is placed inside the enclosure each time the housing is opened and resealed. The desiccant maintains a low internal humidity level throughout operation, protecting the optical surfaces and preserving image quality.
Together, these considerations support reliable operation of the Main Camera  in offshore environments and ensure that both the optical and mechanical components remain stable across a range of environmental conditions.

5.1.7 Electrical and Data Interfaces
This section summarizes the electrical power distribution, data connectivity, bandwidth requirements, and synchronization approach for the visual sensing subsystem. It describes the interfaces as implemented at the subsystem level and provides a unified view of how the stereo camera modules and the Main Camera integrate into the Monitoring Package power and data architecture.
5.1.7.1 Power Distribution
All visual sensing elements operate from the Monitoring Package’s regulated 24 volt DC power distribution system. Power is supplied from the CCB to each camera module through a dedicated subsea whip and bulkhead penetrator.
Each camera housing is electrically isolated and receives power independently. The stereo camera modules draw power directly from the 12, or 24 volt rail and internally regulate voltage as required by the camera electronics. The Main Camera  module similarly receives 12 or 24 volt input but incorporates an internal DC to DC converter to generate the camera’s operating voltage.
5.1.7.2 Data Connectivity and Network Topology
All cameras communicate using Ethernet over twisted pair conductors routed through their respective subsea penetrators, and short subsea whips. Inside the CCB, these Ethernet links terminate at a managed network switch that forms the internal Monitoring Package data backbone.
Each camera appears as an independent Ethernet device on the network, with either static or dynamically assigned IP addresses depending on the host ROV configuration. The internal switch aggregates all camera streams and provides a single uplink to the host ROV network and topside control system.
This architecture supports simultaneous streaming from the Main Camera  and both stereo cameras without requiring dedicated point to point links to the topside system. It also allows additional sensors to be integrated using the same network infrastructure without modification to the visual subsystem interfaces.
5.1.7.3 Video Streaming and Bandwidth Requirements
All cameras stream encoded video using RTSP over Ethernet. Typical bandwidth consumption depends on resolution, frame rate, compression format, and scene content.
The stereo cameras are typically operated at 1080p resolution and frame rates suitable for disparity processing and depth estimation. Under these conditions, each stereo stream consumes approximately 6 to 12 Mbps using H.264 encoding, or approximately 3 to 6 Mbps using H.265.
The Main Camera supports a broader range of operating modes. At 1080p30, typical bandwidth usage is approximately 6 to 12 Mbps with H.264 encoding, and 3 to 6 Mbps with H.265. At 4K30, bandwidth increases to approximately 32 to 50 Mbps using H.264, or 12 to 25 Mbps using H.265.
When operated simultaneously, the total visual subsystem bandwidth remains within the capacity of standard Gigabit Ethernet links, leaving margin for additional sensors and control traffic. Network capacity planning and uplink requirements are addressed at the system level in §5.5.
5.1.7.4 Synchronization and Timing
All visual sensing elements use timestamp based synchronization. Each camera embeds time information into its video stream based on its internal clock. Network Time Protocol is used to align camera clocks to a common reference maintained within the Monitoring Package and host ROV network.
For the stereo cameras, frames are paired based on timestamp alignment and expected frame cadence within each interval. This approach provides sufficient temporal alignment for disparity-based depth estimation.
The Main Camera uses the same timestamp reference, enabling correlation between its video stream, and navigation data. This unified timing model supports synchronized recording and post mission analysis across the full Monitoring Package sensor suite.
5.1.8 Optical Performance and Calibration
Accurate optical performance of the visual sensing subsystem depends on calibration procedures that characterize each camera’s imaging geometry and, in the case of the stereo modules, the fixed geometric relationship between paired views. Calibration ensures that image distortion is corrected and that stereo imagery can be rectified for reliable depth estimation.
Calibration requirements differ between the stereo camera modules and the Main Camera . The stereo modules require both intrinsic and extrinsic calibration to support rectification and disparity computation, while the Main Camera requires only limited intrinsic and color calibration to maintain consistent image quality.
5.1.8.1 Stereo Camera Calibration Overview
Each stereo camera assembly undergoes a two-stage calibration process consisting of intrinsic calibration for each individual camera and extrinsic calibration defining the fixed geometric relationship between the left and right views.
Intrinsic calibration determines the optical parameters of each camera independently, including focal length, principal point, and lens distortion coefficients. These parameters describe how 3D scene points project onto the image sensor and are required to remove lens distortion prior to rectification.
Extrinsic calibration defines the relative position and orientation between the two cameras in the stereo assembly. This includes the translation vector corresponding to the fixed 200 millimeter baseline and the relative rotation between the optical axes. These parameters are essential for stereo rectification and depth estimation and are valid only as long as the mechanical relationship between the two camera housings remains unchanged.
5.1.8.2 Stereo Calibration Procedure
The Stereo calibration is performed using a planar calibration target with known geometry. The target is placed at multiple positions and orientations within the overlapping field of view of both cameras, spanning the intended operating depth range. A set of synchronized image pairs is collected, ensuring that both cameras observe the calibration pattern simultaneously.
From these image pairs, the intrinsic parameters for each camera are computed, followed by the extrinsic parameters describing the fixed relationship between the left and right views. The resulting calibration solution enables rectification of stereo image pairs such that corresponding scene points lie on common epipolar lines, allowing reliable disparity computation and depth estimation.
The calibration process is conducted during system integration and whenever the stereo geometry may have been altered. Calibration results are stored as part of the stereo assembly configuration and are applied consistently during real-time processing and post-mission analysis.
5.1.8.3 Calibration Stability and Recalibration Triggers
Stereo calibration remains valid only while the relative position and orientation between the two camera housings are preserved. Any change to this relationship alters the stereo geometry and invalidates the previously established calibration parameters.
For this reason, the stereo assembly is designed with a rigid mechanical connection that preserves the fixed baseline and optical alignment under normal handling and operation. However, replacing either camera housing, modifying the mounting structure, or otherwise disturbing the stereo assembly requires a full recalibration prior to redeployment.
This requirement is enforced procedurally. Any service action that affects the mechanical relationship between the two stereo cameras mandates repetition of the full intrinsic and extrinsic calibration process before the system is returned to service.
5.1.8.4 Main Camera Calibration Considerations
The Main Camera does not participate in stereo depth estimation and therefore does not require extrinsic calibration relative to other cameras. Its optical calibration requirements are limited to intrinsic characterization and image quality adjustment.
Intrinsic calibration of the Main Camera  is used to characterize lens distortion and confirm the effective field of view when imaging through the acrylic dome port. These parameters may be applied during post-processing if distortion correction is required for measurement or analysis, but are not required for real-time operator viewing.
Color balance, white balance, and exposure behavior are configured through the camera’s internal controls and may be adjusted to suit lighting conditions encountered during deployment. Because the Main Camera uses a fixed lens configuration and a rigid internal mount, recalibration is not required unless the lens position or internal optical alignment is changed.
5.1.8.5 Calibration Data Management
Calibration results for the stereo assembly and Main Camera  are treated as configuration data associated with the visual sensing subsystem. Parameters are archived on the topside Command and Control Computer alongside system metadata so that the correct calibration solution is applied during processing and retained for traceability across missions.
Calibration integrity is verified during integration testing and may be periodically confirmed as part of routine maintenance. 
5.1.8.6 Calibration Workflow
Calibration is performed during system integration and whenever maintenance or handling may have altered the optical geometry of the stereo camera assembly. Events that require evaluation include replacement of either stereo camera housing, modification of the stereo mounting structure, or any mechanical disturbance that could affect the relative position or orientation of the two cameras. If none of these events have occurred, the existing calibration parameters remain valid and continue to be used.

5.1.9 Environmental and Mechanical Design
All visual sensing elements in the Monitoring Package are designed for reliable operation in offshore subsea environments and share a common environmental isolation philosophy. Each camera is housed in an independently sealed, pressure-capable enclosure that provides mechanical protection, environmental isolation, and stable optical alignment under expected deployment conditions. This modular enclosure approach allows individual camera modules to be serviced or replaced without affecting adjacent subsystems.
The stereo camera modules use aluminum pressure housings. The Main Camera is housed in a larger cylindrical aluminum pressure enclosure with custom internal mounting hardware. 
Each camera housing incorporates an optical viewport that also serves as part of the pressure boundary. In all cases, the viewport materials are selected to provide optical clarity and  pressure tolerance in order to isolate internal optics from the external environment.
Environmental sealing for all visual sensing modules relies exclusively on static O-ring seals at housing interfaces and end-cap penetrators. No dynamic seals are used. This approach reduces mechanical complexity and minimizes potential leak paths. The Main Camera housing includes an integrated vacuum port that allows seal integrity to be verified prior to deployment through vacuum hold testing. The stereo camera housings do not include vacuum ports and rely on inspection and assembly procedures to ensure sealing integrity.
Thermal behavior of the visual sensing subsystem is benign. The stereo cameras operate at their native supply voltage and have low internal power dissipation, requiring no dedicated thermal management features. The Main Camera includes an internal power conversion module with an integrated heat sink, but overall heat generation remains modest. For all camera modules, heat is dissipated passively through the housing walls into the surrounding seawater, which serves as the primary thermal sink. No active cooling is required. 
All camera housings are independently sealed and independently serviceable, allowing individual modules to be removed or replaced without compromising the integrity of other enclosures. External viewports are accessible for inspection and cleaning between missions, and biofouling is managed procedurally through routine maintenance. 
5.1.10 Maintainability and Serviceability
The visual sensing subsystem is designed to support straightforward field servicing between deployments with minimal disassembly and without specialized facilities. Maintainability is achieved through modular housings, externally accessible interfaces, and configuration controls that can be adjusted without opening pressure enclosures. Routine service actions are intended to be performed between missions as part of normal system handling and preparation.
All camera viewports are externally accessible and may be inspected and cleaned between missions without removing the camera housings from the Monitoring Package. The camera modules use acrylic viewports that can be cleaned using non-abrasive materials to remove salt residue, particulate matter, or biofouling accumulated during deployment. No in water cleaning or active fouling mitigation mechanisms are required; optical clarity is maintained through routine inspection and cleaning between deployments.
Each camera housing is independently sealed and treated as a self-contained unit for service purposes. Camera  housings are not intended to be opened in the field. Replacement of either stereo housing requires full stereo recalibration prior to redeployment, as described in §5.1.8. 
Electrical interfaces supporting the visual sensing subsystem are accessible when the Monitoring Package is out of the water. Subsea whips and bulkhead penetrators allow camera modules to be disconnected and removed without disturbing adjacent subsystems or opening the CCB. 
Operational configuration of the cameras is performed through network based control interfaces. Exposure, gain, white balance, and related imaging parameters can be adjusted using the supplied camera software without physical access to the housings. This allows imaging behavior to be tuned to lighting conditions and water clarity without requiring mechanical adjustment or redeployment. Optical focus is fixed prior to deployment and does not require adjustment during normal operation.
Together, these features support a maintenance approach in which routine servicing consists of viewport inspection and cleaning, configuration verification, and replacement of complete camera modules only when necessary.

5.2 Lighting Subsystem
5.2.1 Purpose and Functional Role
The lighting subsystem supports the visual sensing subsystem by providing controlled artificial illumination within the inspection zone of the Monitoring Package. Its primary function is to enable reliable image formation for both the stereo camera modules and the Main Camera under offshore subsea conditions where ambient light is limited or absent. The lighting system is not intended for general environmental illumination, but rather to deliver illumination tailored to the optical and geometric requirements of the cameras.
Figure 5-2-1: Lighting


Figure 5-2-1 shows the custom modular LED light head developed for the Monitoring Package. The light is designed as a pressure-tolerant, serviceable module that provides controlled illumination for the stereo cameras and Main Camera while supporting cross-platform integration on multiple host ROVs.
At operational depths and in turbid water, image quality is dependent on illumination geometry. In particular, stereo imaging performance is highly sensitive to lighting conditions. Uneven illumination excessive brightness causes glare and backscatter and can reduce local contrast and degrade feature matchability between left and right views. This effect may be less apparent in monocular imagery, but it directly impacts stereo rectification and depth estimation performance.
The lighting subsystem is therefore integrated with camera placement and optical geometry rather than treated as an independent payload. Light source placement, beam profile selection, and intensity control are defined relative to the camera optical axes and the overlapping stereo field of view to illuminate the inspection corridor without introducing strong specular reflections or non-uniform brightness across the scene. A coordinated arrangement supports stable exposure for the Main Camera and preserves contrast across stereo image pairs, enabling consistent visual interpretation and downstream processing.
In addition to its primary role in supporting imaging performance, the lighting subsystem also contributes to operator situational awareness when the Monitoring Package cameras are used for navigation or alignment tasks. While this function is secondary to its imaging role and does not replace host vehicle navigation lighting, it provides useful visual context when operating in close proximity to mooring lines or other subsea structures.
Adjustable intensity allows illumination levels to be matched to local water conditions, inspection distance, and scene content, reducing unnecessary backscatter while maintaining sufficient contrast for fine feature identification. By treating illumination as a controlled imaging input rather than a fixed output, the lighting subsystem enables consistent visual performance across a wide range of deployment conditions.

5.2.3 System Overview and Architecture
The lighting subsystem is integrated into the Monitoring Package as a distributed set of independent light modules controlled centrally by electronics within the Command and Control Bottle (CCB). The system architecture is designed to provide coordinated illumination aligned with the visual sensing geometry while maintaining simple, modular electrical interfaces between the lights, the CCB, and the host ROV.
In the baseline configuration, the lighting subsystem consists of two primary light modules mounted to illuminate the inspection corridor observed by the stereo camera assembly and the Main Camera. Additional light modules may be installed as optional supplemental illumination to expand angular coverage around the inspection corridor while preserving symmetry relative to the visual sensing geometry. All light modules are positioned relative to the camera fields of view rather than independently, ensuring that illumination is concentrated within the region used for inspection rather than broadly illuminating the surrounding environment.


Figure 5-2-3: Lighting Subsystem Architecture Block Diagram
Figure 5-2-3 illustrates the lighting subsystem architecture within the Monitoring Package. The diagram shows the control computer interface used to adjust light intensity, the routing of control commands through the host ROV communication system, and the role of the CCB in distributing power and generating dimming control signals. Each light module is shown as an independent pressure-capable unit receiving 24-volt DC power and pulse-width-modulated control signals via dedicated subsea whips and penetrators.

Each light module is housed in an independent pressure-capable enclosure and connects to the CCB through a dedicated subsea whip and bulkhead penetrator. The CCB supplies a nominal 24 VDC rail, which falls within the operating voltage range of the selected lighting modules. Electrical regulation and LED drive functions are handled internally by the light modules, and no additional power conversion is required within the Monitoring Package.
Control of light intensity is implemented centrally. Operator commands originate at the control computer, which provides a user interface for adjusting the brightness of individual light modules. These commands are routed through the host ROV communication system to the Monitoring Package CCB. Inside the CCB, control messages are forwarded to a dedicated control microcontroller responsible for interpreting the commands and generating the appropriate dimming signals. Intensity control is conveyed to each light module using pulse-width modulation (PWM) signals transmitted alongside the power conductors within the subsea cables.
5.2.4 Lighting Module Configuration
The lighting subsystem uses custom subsea LED light heads developed for this project to provide controlled artificial illumination for the visual sensing elements of the Monitoring Package. Each light head is a sealed, pressure-capable assembly that establishes the optical output through a fixed lens and window, while electrical power conversion and dimming control are managed by the Monitoring Package electronics.
Light output is generated by an internal LED emitter array integrated into each light module and sized to meet the illuminance and beam-coverage targets in DCR §2.2. The CCB power distribution provides DC power while the CCB microcontroller provides dimming control signal,the light module converts commanded intensity into regulated LED drive current. This approach reduces system-level electrical complexity and supports straightforward replacement of individual light modules during service.
Lighting modules attach to the Monitoring Package frame using mechanical mounting interfaces that establish fixed position and orientation relative to the stereo camera assembly and Main Camera. These interfaces allow individual light heads to be removed and replaced without disturbing adjacent subsystems. Electrical power and control signals are delivered to each module through dedicated subsea whips and bulkhead penetrators, as described in §5.2.3.
This approach establishes a well-defined electrical, mechanical, and optical interface between the lighting modules and the Monitoring Package while preserving consistent illumination behavior across deployments.
5.2.5 Placement and Geometric Relationship to Cameras
Lighting module placement is defined relative to the geometry of the visual sensing subsystem rather than treated as an independent payload. In the baseline configuration, two lighting modules are mounted to the same structural frame as the stereo cameras and Main Camera, positioned laterally offset and above the camera plane. This placement establishes intentional separation between illumination and imaging axes while maintaining a stable geometric relationship across all visual sensors.
The lighting modules are oriented toward the intended inspection corridor at a working distance of approximately 1 to 5 meters. Each light is aimed with a fixed inward toe-in of approximately 15 degrees and produces a circular illumination beam with a nominal full angle of approximately 80 degrees. This configuration causes the illuminated volumes to intersect the camera fields of view ahead of the lenses, rather than illuminating the near field immediately in front of the optics.

Stereo imaging imposes additional geometric constraints on lighting placement. All lighting modules are positioned entirely outside the stereo baseline and are not located between the stereo camera housings. With the baseline configuration of two modules, lighting placement is symmetric about the vehicle centerline, resulting in similar illumination conditions for the left and right stereo views. If optional additional lighting modules are installed, placement may be extended symmetrically above and below the camera plane while preserving left-right balance.
Angular separation between illumination and imaging axes reduces direct backscatter from suspended particulates and limits specular reflections into the camera lenses. These effects are addressed through placement and orientation. Structural elements of the Monitoring Package frame are designed and positioned to avoid interference with the primary illumination cones, preventing unintended reflections within the camera fields of view.
5.2.6 Electrical Design and Driver Architecture
The lighting subsystem is powered directly from the Monitoring Package’s regulated DC distribution provided by the CCB. Each LED light module receives a nominal 24 VDC supply via a dedicated subsea whip and penetrator. Constant-current LED regulation, dimming, and protection functions are implemented within the light module electronics.
Lighting modules are powered in a star topology rather than a daisy-chained topology. Each module receives power independently from the CCB , supporting electrical isolation between lights and simplifying fault identification and service actions. A failure or disconnection of one lighting module does not affect power delivery to the remaining modules.
Functions required for safe and stable LED operation including constant-current regulation, current limiting, and fault protection are provided within each light module electronics. The system-level interface to each light head is therefore limited to DC power and a dimming control signal, reducing complexity in the Monitoring Package and supporting modular replacement.
Individual lighting modules draw approximately 24 to 120 watts depending on commanded intensity, allowing aggregate lighting power to remain within the envelope defined in DCR §2.2.
This electrical architecture provides a straightforward and robust interface between the Monitoring Package electronics and the lighting subsystem. By keeping constant-current regulation, protection, and thermal limiting within each light module, and limiting the Monitoring Package interface to DC power, the design reduces field service complexity while maintaining compatibility with the performance and environmental requirements defined for offshore deployment.
5.2.7 Control Interfaces and Intensity Modulation
Operational control of the lighting subsystem is implemented to support consistent imaging performance across varying environmental conditions rather than to provide general illumination control. Lighting intensity is adjusted to maintain usable exposure for the Main Camera  and to preserve image contrast and feature matchability for the stereo camera system as operating distance, water clarity, and lighting conditions change during a mission.
Lighting intensity modulation is accomplished through driver-level control generated within the CCB Microcontroller. The microcontroller commands the LED driver stage to adjust output smoothly and repeatably, enabling real-time tuning of illumination to match turbidity, standoff distance, and camera exposure settings.
Intensity commands will originate from the operator interface and are conveyed through the Monitoring Package control system to the lighting modules. The control architecture supports independent adjustment of individual lights as well as coordinated changes across multiple modules. This allows illumination to be tuned to the active camera geometry and inspection task without altering the physical configuration of the lighting hardware.
Lighting control emphasizes smooth and continuous adjustment rather than discrete stepped changes. This consideration is particularly relevant for stereo imaging, where abrupt changes in illumination can affect disparity matching before similar effects are apparent in monocular imagery.
Repeatable intensity settings are supported to limit illumination-induced variability in recorded imagery. By maintaining consistent lighting states across deployments, the system preserves stereo calibration assumptions and improves the comparability of datasets used for post-mission review and future automated analysis.
5.2.8 Thermal Behavior and Environmental Considerations
Thermal behavior is managed through conservative drive limits, internal temperature sensing in the driver electronics, and passive conduction through the light head housings into the surrounding seawater. If thermal limits are approached, the system reduces output or disables the affected channel to protect the hardware.
Heat generated within the lighting modules is conducted through the pressure housing and dissipated into the surrounding seawater, which serves as the primary thermal sink. No active cooling or additional thermal management features are required at the system level. The thermal load imposed by the lighting modules is not expected to introduce localized heating concerns for adjacent components or structural elements within the Monitoring Package.
The lighting components are designed for the project operating envelope, including subsea pressures consistent with the Monitoring Package depth rating and seawater temperatures expected during MoorSEA inspection missions. 
Overall, thermal behavior and environmental exposure of the lighting subsystem are bounded by the characteristics of the selected hardware and the subsea operating environment, and do not impose additional thermal management or operational constraints on the Monitoring Package.
5.2.9 Maintainability and Serviceability
The lighting subsystem is designed to support routine servicing between deployments without disassembly of pressure housings or exposure of internal components. All maintenance actions are confined to work that can be performed externally or resolved through replacement of a complete lighting module.
All lighting module optical windows are externally accessible and may be inspected and cleaned between missions using non-abrasive methods. Routine cleaning removes accumulated residue or surface fouling that could affect illumination quality. No in-water cleaning or active mitigation mechanisms are required, and normal maintenance consists of inspection and cleaning performed as part of standard system handling procedures.
Lighting modules are treated as sealed, self-contained units and are not intended to be opened in the field. If a lighting module experiences a fault or degradation that cannot be resolved through external handling, the complete unit is removed and replaced. This service model avoids internal access or repair activities and preserves the integrity of pressure boundaries and internal electronics.
Each lighting module connects to the Monitoring Package through a dedicated subsea whip and bulkhead penetrator, allowing individual units to be electrically disconnected and removed without affecting other lighting modules or visual sensing components. Replacement of a lighting module does not require modification to internal wiring within the CCB.
Together, these design choices support a maintenance approach limited to external handling and full module replacement, reducing the likelihood of introducing sealing or wiring faults during routine maintenance.

5.3 Sonar Subsystem
5.3.1 Purpose and Functional Role
The sonar subsystem provides complementary acoustic sensing, implemented using a forward-looking 2D imaging sonar. It supplies spatial context ahead of the vehicle in conditions where camera-based sensing alone may provide limited or incomplete information.
The primary function of the sonar subsystem is to support operator situational awareness during inspection activities. Forward-looking acoustic imagery provides standoff context during approach to mooring lines and nearby structures, allowing operators to assess general geometry and relative position prior to entering the effective working range of the visual sensors. This spatial context supports controlled approaches and informed interpretation of subsequent camera imagery.
The sonar subsystem complements the cameras in the visual sensing subsystem. Cameras remain the primary means of detailed inspection and identification of secondary entanglements, with sonar used to inform operator understanding.

5.3.4 Sonar Module Description

5.3.5 Placement and Geometric Relationship to Other Sensors
The sonar module is rigidly mounted to the Monitoring Package in a forward-looking orientation and aligned with the primary camera optical axes. The mounting configuration is fixed, with no articulation, ensuring a stable and repeatable geometric relationship between the acoustic and visual sensing elements.
This alignment is intended to establish a shared forward inspection corridor between the sonar and camera subsystems. By observing the same general region of space ahead of the vehicle, the sonar provides forward spatial context that complements subsequent visual inspection of mooring lines and surrounding structures. This co-aligned perspective supports clearer interpretation of sonar imagery relative to camera views during approach and inspection.
5.3.6 Electrical and Data Interfaces
The sonar subsystem interfaces electrically and digitally with the Monitoring Package as an external networked sensor. Electrical power is supplied from the Monitoring Package power distribution system via the Command and Control Bottle (CCB) , and sonar data is transmitted digitally to the topside command and control computer over the host ROV network.
The selected P900 sonar accepts a DC input voltage in the range of 12–48 VDC, allowing it to be powered directly from the Monitoring Package nominal 24 VDC distribution rail. Typical power consumption is approximately 18 W, with peak consumption on the order of 22 W under maximum operating conditions. 
Sonar data is transmitted over a standard Ethernet interface. Acoustic image data is streamed continuously when the sonar is enabled and routed through the CCB internal network switch, the host ROV communication system, and onward to the topside command and control computer. 
5.3.7 Control, Configuration, and Operating Modes
Operation of the sonar subsystem is controlled by the operator through manufacturer-provided sonar software running on the topside command and control computer. No direct control or mode management of the sonar is implemented within the subsea electronics or the Monitoring Package itself.
The sonar supports real-time viewing of acoustic imagery during inspection operations and provides the option for operator-initiated data recording through the manufacturer software environment. Configuration settings and operating mode selection are performed entirely within the vendor software, and no custom control logic or supervisory functions are implemented as part of the Monitoring Package design.
5.3.8 Environmental Considerations

The P900 product family includes multiple variants with differing depth capabilities, allowing the sonar configuration to be selected in accordance with mission-specific environmental constraints while preserving a consistent integration approach. This modularity ensures that the Monitoring Package sonar subsystem can be configured to operate across the full range of intended deployment environments without changes to the underlying system architecture.
5.3.9 Maintainability and Serviceability

Maintenance and servicing are performed through module-level replacement. The sonar is integrated such that it can be removed and replaced without disturbing internal electronics or other subsystems within the Monitoring Package.

5.4 Navigation and Positioning Subsystem
5.4.1 Purpose and Functional Role
The navigation and positioning subsystem provides the spatial awareness required to support effective inspection of floating offshore wind mooring systems by the MoorSEA Monitoring Package. Mooring structures are spatially complex, extend over large vertical and horizontal distances, and are frequently deployed in deep water environments where external visual reference cues are limited. Under these conditions, reliable spatial awareness is necessary to interpret inspection observations and to maintain consistent operational context during monitoring activities.
The Monitoring Package is designed under the operational assumptions defined in DCR §1.6, which specify that the host ROV provides only basic locomotion, depth capability, power delivery, and a communication link. Consistent with these assumptions, navigation and positioning capabilities are integrated into the Monitoring Package as a self-contained sensing function. This design approach allows the Monitoring Package to be deployed across a broad range of ROV platforms with varying onboard instrumentation while maintaining consistent navigation performance.
Navigation and positioning data provide spatial context for sensor observations collected by the Monitoring Package during inspection operations. This context supports interpretation of observed features relative to mooring components and surrounding structures. It also enables operators to assess inspection coverage and proximity during monitoring passes. Navigation data are used to support operator awareness and interpretation of inspection activities, and are not used to command or control vehicle motion.
Navigation and positioning information also support inspection repeatability across deployments. By associating observations with spatial reference information, the system enables reinspection of specific mooring segments and comparison of inspection results over time. This capability supports documentation of potential entanglements. The navigation and positioning framework preserves spatial context across missions.
In addition, navigation and positioning data provide the basis for spatially referenced data products generated during post mission analysis. Recorded imagery, sonar data, and inspection observations can be associated with physical locations along the mooring system, supporting reporting and review activities.
The navigation and positioning subsystem is intended to support operator situational awareness and data interpretation. It does not implement any vehicle locomotion or piloting function.  Responsibility for vehicle control remains with the host ROV, consistent with the division of responsibilities established in the DCR.

5.4.3 System Overview and Architecture
The navigation and positioning subsystem is implemented as a distributed system that combines information from surface based reference sensors and subsea navigation sensors to describe the location and orientation of the Monitoring Package during inspection operations, with final aggregation performed at the topside command and control computer.
Figure 5-4-3: Navigation System Block Diagram
Figure 5-4-3 shows the navigation and positioning subsystem architecture and the functional relationships between surface based reference components, subsea navigation sensors associated with the Monitoring Package, the subsea electronics interface, and the topside command and control computer. 
Surface based components establish the external reference needed to locate the Monitoring Package in geographic space. The surface GPS receiver provides the position of the surface platform, and the surface heading reference defines the orientation of that platform. The selected Furuno GP-39 is a time synchronized GPS system with sub-meter accuracy.  The Ultra Short Baseline (USBL) system measures the relative direction and distance between the surface platform and the Monitoring Package through acoustic signals exchanged with a subsea transponder. The selected TrackPoint 3 System satisfies the range requirements defined in NR-3. These surface measurements define the position of the Monitoring Package relative to the surface platform.
Subsea navigation sensors describe the local state of the Monitoring Package itself. The selected NaviGuider compass provides the heading of the package and includes an integrated  rate gyroscope that  tracks short term changes in orientation. The compass utilizes an internal Sensor Fusion algorithm that integrates gyroscope data with compass data to produce a heading with less than 1 degree heading accuracy.  The chosen depth sensor indicates vertical position in the water column, up to 2000m depth. Together these sensors describe how the Monitoring Package is oriented and positioned locally while inspections are being conducted.
Navigation measurements from surface and subsea elements are transmitted through the Monitoring Package and the host ROV communication system to the topside command and control computer.
At the topside command and control computer, navigation data from multiple sources are time aligned and combined into a single, consistent navigation description. This navigation information is presented to the operator and associated with recorded inspection data. The navigation and positioning subsystem supports operator awareness and spatial interpretation of inspection activities.

5.4.5 Surface Based Positioning Components
Surface-based positioning components establish the external geographic reference frame required to determine the absolute position of the Monitoring Package during inspection operations. 
Surface position is provided by a Furuno GP-39 GPS Navigator. The GP-39 delivers sub-meter class positioning accuracy and provides a time-synchronized geodetic reference for the surface platform. This GPS position defines the origin for acoustic positioning using the Ultra Short Baseline system and serves as the global reference frame for subsea position estimation.
Surface heading is provided by a KVH AutoComp 1000 digital compass. The AutoComp 1000 supplies heading information (with less than 2° error) referenced to geographic north and is mechanically aligned with the USBL hydrophone array. Accurate heading input is required to resolve the azimuth component of acoustic measurements and directly influences horizontal position accuracy derived from the USBL solution.
Absolute subsea positioning is performed by the TrackPoint 3 USBL system, which is integrated into the topside command and control architecture. The TrackPoint 3 transceiver emits acoustic interrogation signals and receives responses from the subsea USBL transponder mounted on the Monitoring Package. By measuring slant range and bearing relative to the surface heading reference, the TrackPoint system computes the position of the Monitoring Package relative to the surface platform. When combined with the GPS position of the vessel, this yields an absolute geographic estimate of Monitoring Package position.
The Furuno GP-39, KVH AutoComp 1000, and TrackPoint 3 system therefore operate together as a unified topside navigation subsystem, defining the global reference frame within which all subsea inspection observations are spatially interpreted.
5.4.6 Subsea Based Navigation Sensors
Subsea navigation sensors provide the local state description of the Monitoring Package during inspection operations. These sensors are mounted on or integrated within the Monitoring Package structure and/or CCB and operate independently of host ROV navigation instrumentation. The selected subsea navigation suite consists of a PNI NaviGuider compass module, a TrackPoint 3 compatible USBL transponder, and a Keller D-line Series 4LD pressure-based depth sensor.
Heading and short-term orientation stability are provided by the PNI NaviGuider. The NaviGuider integrates a magnetic compass, rate gyroscope, and onboard sensor fusion within a single module. It supports automatic hard-iron and soft-iron calibration, reducing offshore calibration burden and improving robustness in the presence of nearby metallic structures. The integrated gyroscope provides angular rate measurements, with 0.1° per second accuracy,  that stabilizes heading estimates during dynamic motion and transient magnetic disturbances, resulting in a final heading accuracy of less than 1° error. Sensor fusion is performed internally within the NaviGuider, producing a stabilized heading output suitable for real-time operator awareness and data logging. The NaviGuider connects to the CCB microcontroller which bridges its data over the ethernet network. 
Relative subsea positioning is supported by a TrackPoint 3 compatible USBL transponder mounted on the Monitoring Package. The transponder responds to interrogation signals from the surface TrackPoint 3 transceiver, enabling slant range and bearing estimation. When combined with surface GPS and heading inputs, this provides an absolute geographic position estimate for the Monitoring Package.
Vertical position is measured using a Keller D-line Series 4LD piezoresistive pressure transmitter. The sensor employs chip-in-oil technology, with the silicon sensing element oil-coupled and isolated by a stainless steel diaphragm to ensure long-term stability and corrosion resistance. The sensor provides 16-bit pressure and temperature data via an I²C interface, allowing direct digital integration with the CCB  microcontroller. It is mounted through a stainless steel M10 × 1.5 bulkhead fitting, sealed with an O-ring so that the sensing diaphragm is exposed to ambient hydrostatic pressure. Depth reference is established by atmospheric zeroing prior to deployment.
Together, the NaviGuider, USBL transponder, and Keller depth sensor provide heading, absolute position, and vertical reference information required to satisfy the subsea navigation requirements defined in DCR §2.4.
5.4.7 Optional Navigation Enhancements
Optional navigation enhancements identified in DCR §2.4.3 may be integrated depending on mission context and host ROV capability.
A Doppler Velocity Log (DVL) provides velocity measurements relative to the seabed or water column. When operating in bottom-lock mode, a DVL supports dead-reckoning estimation and improves short-term positional stability between USBL fixes. Our current design leaves the integration of a DVL as a future item to be addressed.
An altimeter provides direct measurement of distance to nearby surfaces, supporting consistent standoff control relative to mooring components or the seabed. Currently we include an optional altimeter as an addition to our stereo camera system defined in §5.1.4.
Visual odometry methods use image-based feature tracking or optical flow to estimate motion relative to observed structures. This capability may supplement inertial and acoustic measurements in environments where sufficient visual texture exists.
These enhancements are not required to meet baseline DCR navigation requirements and are treated as optional extensions, and are left for future design projects.
5.4.8 Placement and Coordinate Frames
Navigation sensor placement and coordinate alignment are defined relative to the Monitoring Package body frame. A right-handed body-fixed coordinate frame is assumed, with the forward inspection direction defining the positive longitudinal axis, starboard defining the lateral axis, and downward defining the vertical axis.
Navigation sensors are mounted with this known orientation relative to this body frame. Accurate interpretation of heading, pitch, and roll requires that sensor axes be aligned with or mapped to the body frame through known rotational transformations.
The USBL transponder is positioned to minimize acoustic shadowing from structural elements and to maintain consistent acoustic exposure during inspection operations. The depth sensor is positioned such that it experiences ambient hydrostatic pressure, through the CCB endcap.
5.4.9 Electrical and Data Interfaces
Navigation sensors interface electrically and logically with the Monitoring Package through defined power and communication channels. Power for subsea navigation sensors is supplied from the regulated 24 VDC distribution system described in Chapter 4. Sensors requiring alternate operating voltages incorporate internal or intermediate regulation as required. Data interfaces are implemented using standard communication classes appropriate to each sensor type. 
Navigation data are transmitted through the Monitoring Package communication backbone and relayed to the topside command and control computer for aggregation and storage.
The PNI NaviGuider connects to the CCB microcontroller via UART Serial and provides heading and attitude data at 15Hz (configurable). The Keller depth sensor interfaces with the CCB microcontroller via I²C, providing 16-bit pressure and temperature data. The USBL transponder communicates via sonar with the topside TrackPoint 3 system, which is connected directly to the topside command and control computer. The USBL transponder uses a built-in long lasting internal battery. All other sensors receive 24 VDC power from the CCB distribution system, with onboard regulation where required.
5.4.10 Time Synchronization and Data Alignment
All navigation sensor data are timestamped by the topside command and control computer upon receipt and recording. This approach establishes a common time reference across all data streams without requiring synchronized clocks or hardware timestamping capability in the subsea sensors themselves.
Timestamping at the topside computer ensures consistent temporal alignment between navigation measurements and inspection sensor data (video, sonar, etc.). This alignment enables accurate spatial referencing of observations during post-mission analysis and supports correlation of inspection findings with specific locations along the mooring system.
No additional time synchronization hardware or protocols are required within the subsea Monitoring Package to support this function.
5.4.11 Sensor Calibration 
The PNI NaviGuider performs automated magnetic calibration during startup and continuously adjusts for hard-iron and soft-iron effects during operation. No manual compensation procedures are required.
Depth sensor calibration consists of atmospheric zeroing prior to deployment. Factory calibration from the sensor manufacturer establishes baseline performance characteristics.
5.4.12 Environmental and Mechanical Considerations
Navigation sensors operate in a high-pressure subsea environment with variable temperature and the presence of large metallic structures. The depth sensor is intentionally exposed to ambient hydrostatic pressure through a sealed bulkhead interface, while inertial and magnetic sensors are housed within pressure-capable enclosures.These housings provide environmental isolation consistent with the depth and temperature limits established in DCR §2.4.
Temperature variation may influence sensor bias and drift, particularly for inertial and magnetic devices. The depth sensor and compass both incorporate internal temperature compensation.  Heat dissipation is passive, with no active thermal control required.
Magnetic interference is the primary environmental sensitivity affecting heading accuracy. Structural metals, power conductors, and nearby electrical components may introduce distortion. Compass placement inside the CCB therefore prioritizes mechanical stability and separation from high-current elements, and calibration compensates for residual hard-iron and soft-iron effects. Inertial sensors are rigidly mounted within the bottle to preserve fixed alignment with the Monitoring Package body frame, as relative motion would degrade heading interpretation. The USBL transponder is positioned to minimize acoustic shadowing from ROV or Monitoring Package Structures.  These mechanical and environmental controls support navigation accuracy consistent with DCR §2.4.
5.4.13 Maintainability and Serviceability
Navigation components are integrated as modular elements with defined mechanical and electrical interfaces. Individual sensors may be accessed or replaced without disassembly of unrelated subsystems.
Replacement of inertial or magnetic sensors requires recalibration to restore alignment and heading accuracy, however the selected sensor provides automated calibration, requiring no action from the end user.  Depth measurement requires establishment of a surface reference prior to each deployment but does not require structural recalibration unless the mounting interface changes. USBL calibration is performed at the system installation level.
Service actions are limited to inspection and modular replacement and not focused on  in-field component repair. Navigation maintenance does not affect propulsion or host ROV control functions.

5.5 Communication Subsystem
5.5.1 Purpose and Functional Role
The Communication Subsystem implements a distributed IP network architecture in which each Monitoring Package subsystem presents network endpoints to the topside command and control computer. This includes bidirectional communication supporting real time operator awareness, system configuration, subsystem control commands, health monitoring, and mission data logging.
The subsystem establishes Ethernet as the primary communications backbone. Subsea devices that communicate via serial or other non-Ethernet interfaces are integrated through the Command and Control Bottle (CCB) microcontroller, which aggregates and relays their data to the topside command and control computer over Ethernet. The Communication Subsystem provides a consistent and platform-independent interface between Monitoring Package components regardless of host ROV implementation details. It ensures that all Monitoring Package data streams are accessible to the topside command and control computer using TCP/IP-based communication mechanisms.

5.5.3 System Overview and Architecture
The Communication Subsystem is implemented as a distributed Ethernet-based network architecture spanning the subsea Command and Control Bottle (CCB), the host ROV transport link, and the topside command and control computer. The system uses Ethernet as the primary backbone, with fiber as the subsea-to-surface physical transport medium. The host ROV network is treated as a transparent transport layer linking subsea and topside elements.
Within the Command and Control Bottle, a BotBlox industrial Ethernet switch serves as the central subsea network node. All Ethernet-capable subsea subsystems connect directly to this switch. These include imaging systems, sonar systems, navigation components with native Ethernet interfaces, and any additional IP-based devices integrated into the Monitoring Package. Each subsystem presents standardized TCP/IP endpoints accessible from the topside command and control computer.
Subsea devices that do not natively support Ethernet, including serial, PWM, or analog-output components such as the lighting controller and depth sensor, are interfaced through the CCB microcontroller. The microcontroller functions as a bridge, converting serial, analog, and low-level control signals into IP-based network traffic. Converted data streams are injected into the subsea Ethernet network through the BotBlox switch, ensuring that all Monitoring Package data appear as standardized network-accessible endpoints.
The subsea Ethernet switch connects to the host ROV via a fiber-only uplink. This fiber connection carries all Monitoring Package traffic, including video streams, sonar data, navigation telemetry, system health information, and command traffic. The host ROV network transports this data to the surface without modification or interpretation.

5.5.6 Subsea Communications Interfaces
Within the subsea domain, Ethernet-capable subsystems—including the Main Camera, stereo cameras, and sonar—connect directly to the BotBlox fiber switch, which serves as the central network node inside the Command and Control Bottle (see Figure 5-5-3). These devices communicate using standard Ethernet connections and present IP-accessible endpoints to the topside system.
Subsea sensors that do not natively support Ethernet, such as the depth sensor (I²C) and lighting controllers (PWM), connect to the CCB microcontroller using their native interfaces. The microcontroller converts these signals into IP-based data streams and relays them into the subsea Ethernet network through the fiber switch.
5.5.7 Data Transport Model
The Communication Subsystem implements a distributed network model in which each Monitoring Package subsystem exposes data streams or services accessible to the topside command and control computer. Subsystems present standardized network endpoints over the Ethernet backbone, allowing bidirectional command and telemetry exchange.
TCP/IP is used where reliable, session-based communication is required, such as configuration, control commands, and structured data transfer. UDP may be used for telemetry-style streams, including compressed video or high-rate sensor data, where low-latency is prioritized and limited packet loss is acceptable. Subsea serial, analog, or PWM sources are normalized into structured network messages by the CCB microcontroller before being injected into the Ethernet network.
5.5.8 Protocol and Message Conventions
The Communication Subsystem employs a consistent internal convention for identifying data streams and structuring messages exchanged between subsea subsystems and the topside command and control computer. This convention defines how subsystem data are represented and transported over the Ethernet backbone.
Specific addressing methods, stream naming schemes, message framing structures, and session management behaviors will be defined during software implementation and are not fixed at the architectural level described in this report.
5.5.9 Bandwidth and Prioritization
The Communication Subsystem supports multiple concurrent traffic classes, including video streams, sonar data, navigation telemetry, system status information, command messages, and logged data. The Ethernet backbone and fiber uplink are selected to provide sufficient aggregate throughput for these data types under expected operating conditions.
Based on the video encoding requirements defined in CR-3 (H.264/H.265 at >4 Mbps per stream), the three cameras generate a combined video bandwidth of approximately 12-15 Mbps assuming moderate compression overhead. Sonar data adds approximately 1-2 Mbps, and navigation telemetry, system status, and command traffic collectively require less than 1 Mbps under normal operation. Total aggregate throughput is therefore estimated at 15-20 Mbps, which is well within the capacity of the Gigabit Ethernet backbone and fiber uplink.
The BotBlox switch supports Quality of Service (QoS) features that enable prioritization of critical traffic. Command and control messages are configured for higher priority handling to preserve operator responsiveness, even during peak video or sonar data streaming. Specific bandwidth allocation and queue management strategies are implementation-dependent and may be tuned during integration testing, but the underlying infrastructure provides the necessary mechanisms to support prioritization.
5.5.10 Time Synchronization Support
The Communication Subsystem preserves timestamp information generated by subsea subsystems during transport to the topside command and control computer. Transport mechanisms do not alter or reinterpret time metadata. Final time alignment and cross-sensor correlation occur at the command and control computer during data processing and logging.
5.5.11 Environmental and Mechanical Considerations
Communication interfaces within the Monitoring Package are implemented using subsea-rated connectors and sealed penetrators appropriate for offshore deployment. Internal cable routing within the Command and Control Bottle (CCB) provides strain relief and maintains minimum bend radius requirements, particularly for fiber-optic connections. Fiber is used for the host ROV uplink to the BotBlox switch, reducing susceptibility to electromagnetic interference and supporting high-bandwidth data transport. Other internal device connections within the CCB use copper cabling. These measures support reliable data transport in the subsea operating environment. 
5.5.12 Maintainability and Serviceability
The Communication Subsystem is organized around modular components, which may be replaced independently without disassembly of unrelated subsystems. Clear interface boundaries between sensors, the CCB microcontroller relay, the Ethernet switch, and the host network support fault isolation during troubleshooting. Service actions are limited to modular replacement rather than in-field component repair.

5.6 Command and Control Computer (Topside)
The Command and Control Computer is the topside aggregation, operator interface, and data logging node for the MoorSEA Monitoring Package. All subsea subsystems communicate to this computer over the host ROV Ethernet network. The computer hosts the monitoring software, provides real time situational awareness, and records mission data including video, sonar, navigation, and system telemetry.
5.6.1 Network Interfaces
The computer includes:
One fiber capable Ethernet interface for connection to the host ROV network.
One secondary Ethernet interface for connection to the wider MoorSEA system.
RS 232 connectivity for non-Ethernet topside devices, provided either through native serial ports or USB to RS 232 adapters included in the deployed configuration.


5.6.2 Compute and Memory Provisioning
The system is provisioned to simultaneously decode, display, and record three compressed video streams (two stereo cameras and one Main Camera) at the maximum operational resolution and frame rate used by the Monitoring Package.
Minimum compute and memory provisioning is defined as:
CPU: Multi core processor with a minimum of 8 physical cores (or equivalent performance class) capable of sustaining simultaneous hardware accelerated decode of three H.264 or H.265 streams while supporting logging and operator interface processes without frame loss.
System Memory: Minimum 32 GB RAM to support concurrent video buffering, sensor telemetry handling, operator interface rendering, and file system caching without paging.
Graphics Processing: Hardware video decode acceleration support for H.264 and H.265 to ensure stable multi stream playback and display.
These minimums are sized to prevent compute contention during real time operation and continuous recording.
5.6.3 Storage Provisioning
The system is provisioned to record at least 24 hours of mission data without offload.

250 GB usable storage capacity dedicated to mission recording

Chapter 6: Interface Definitions
This chapter defines the external mechanical, electrical, optical, and logical interfaces between the MoorSEA ROV Monitoring Package and a compatible Host ROV platform. It establishes the formal boundary conditions at which the Monitoring Package connects to the host vehicle.
The specifications presented herein implement the Physical and Electrical Interface Requirements defined in DCR §2.6. This chapter translates those requirements into measurable interface definitions that enable platform-independent integration.
The intent of this chapter is to define the integration interface between systems. It identifies what the Host ROV must provide, what the Monitoring Package will accept or deliver, and the constraints governing those interactions. These definitions are expressed in terms of voltage ranges, communication standards, connector types, mounting geometry, dimensional envelopes, and data exchange formats.
This chapter does not restate the functional or hardware architecture described in Chapter 2, nor does it reintroduce subsystem design rationale presented elsewhere in this report. 
Together, the sections that follow provide the complete specification required for mechanical mounting, power provisioning, network integration, and data exchange between the Monitoring Package and a Host ROV platform.
6.1 Interface Boundary Definition
For integration purposes, the Monitoring Package is defined as the Command and Control Bottle (CCB), all electronics housed within it, all external sensor and illumination modules supplied with the system, the subsea whips connecting those modules to the CCB, the integrated mounting framework, and the topside command and control computer. These components together form a single delivered system.
The Host ROV provides a subsea DC power source, a fiber-optic transport path through the ROV umbilical, and a mechanical structure that supports attachment of the Monitoring Package. The Host ROV network infrastructure serves as a physical transport medium for Ethernet traffic between subsea and topside but does not modify or interpret Monitoring Package data.
The interface between the Monitoring Package and the Host ROV exists at multiple levels. It includes the electrical boundary at the DC power entry, the optical Ethernet boundary at the subsea fiber connection, the mechanical boundary at the mounting attachment features, and the logical boundary defined by transport protocols and message formats transmitted across the Ethernet link.
All interface requirements defined in this chapter apply at these boundaries between the Monitoring Package and the Host ROV. Internal subsystem interfaces within the Monitoring Package are outside the scope of this chapter and are documented in Chapter 2 and the supporting appendices.

6.3 External Electrical and Optical Interface
This section defines the DC power and optical Ethernet interfaces between the Monitoring Package and the Host ROV. These definitions establish the electrical and optical boundaries through which power and data are exchanged during operation.
6.3.1 Power Interface
The Monitoring Package subsea hardware assembly is designed to operate from a nominal 24-48 VDC or 120-240VAC supply provided by the Host ROV at the subsea interface.
The maximum continuous power draw of the subsea Monitoring Package hardware shall not exceed 450 W under full operational load. The Host ROV power distribution system shall be sized to support this continuous load at  24-48 VDC or 120-240VAC.
Within the Monitoring Package subsea assembly, the DC negative conductor is bonded to chassis, establishing the electrical reference at the host interface.
At the surface, the Monitoring Package topside command and control computer requires 120 VAC power. The Host ROV topside shall provide a 120 VAC supply suitable for continuous operation of the topside system.
Connector pin assignments and interface termination details are provided in Appendix B.
6.3.2 Optical Ethernet Interface
The Monitoring Package uses Gigabit Ethernet over fiber to transmit data between the subsea hardware assembly and the Monitoring Package topside command and control computer.
At the subsea boundary, the Monitoring Package provides an ST optical connector. The Host ROV shall provide a compatible fiber transport path through the vehicle umbilical and an SFP transceiver configured for 1510 nm transmit and 1470 nm receive operation to establish bidirectional communication.
At the topside boundary, the Monitoring Package command and control computer provides an LC duplex SFP interface. The Host ROV shall provide a compatible SFP transceiver configured for 1470 nm transmit and 1510 nm receive operation to complete the optical link.
The Host ROV fiber infrastructure functions solely as a transport path between subsea and topside.
6.4 External Data and Communication Interface
6.4.1 Network Layer
The Monitoring Package communicates between subsea and topside using IP networking over the Host ROV provided Ethernet transport path. The Host ROV network implementation is treated as an internal detail of the host platform and is not constrained by the Monitoring Package, provided it supports end to end packet delivery between Monitoring Package endpoints.
The Monitoring Package uses an internal IP address space that is independent of any address space used by the Host ROV for vehicle functions. The Host ROV shall provide a transparent network path that allows Monitoring Package subsea devices and the Monitoring Package topside command and control computer to exchange IP traffic without requiring coordination of addressing schemes.
The Host ROV network path shall support jumbo frame operation with an MTU of at least 9000 bytes for Monitoring Package traffic. All Monitoring Package network traffic is unicast.
6.4.2 Transport Layer
Monitoring Package traffic may utilize connectionless or connection-oriented transport protocols as required by subsystem functionality. The Host ROV network infrastructure shall permit transparent forwarding of both TCP and UDP traffic between subsea and topside endpoints.
Transport port assignments are configurable at integration time and are not fixed by this specification. The Host ROV shall not restrict Monitoring Package communication based on port number, provided traffic remains within the operational bandwidth limits of the host network.
Video transport formats may vary by deployment and may include UDP-based streaming mechanisms. The Host ROV network path shall support sustained high-bandwidth unicast traffic.
6.4.3 Logical Data Schema Definition
This section defines the logical message structure used for non-video data produced by the Monitoring Package. Any system that intends to receive, process, or generate Monitoring Package non-video data shall conform to the schema defined herein.
Non video Monitoring Package data shall be encoded as UTF 8 JSON. Each message shall be conveyed as a single JSON object contained within one UDP datagram.
All systems that generate or consume Monitoring Package non-video messages shall support the required top level schema shown below:
json
{
  "schema_version": version#,

  "source": "sensor_id",
  "type": "data_type",
  "data": {
    // JSON Object
  }
}

The schema_version field shall be an integer identifying the global version of the Monitoring Package message schema. The source field shall be a string identifying the originating subsystem or device. The type field shall be a string identifying the message category. The data field shall be a JSON object containing the message payload.
Numeric values shall be represented as JSON numbers. Units shall follow SI conventions unless otherwise specified within the definition of a given message type.
The maximum UDP payload size for any JSON message shall not exceed 8192 bytes.
Specific payload structures for individual message types are outside the scope of this report.
6.4.4 Video Streaming Interface
Video streams produced by the Monitoring Package are made available via RTSP. Any system that intends to receive Monitoring Package video shall support standard RTSP session control and associated RTP media transport.
Each video stream is encoded using either H.264 or H.265, depending on camera configuration. Receiving systems shall support decoding of at least one of these encodings to ensure compatibility.
Up to four concurrent RTSP streams may be available from the Monitoring Package. Access to these streams is optional and dependent on system configuration.
Video bitrate is configurable and may range from approximately 4 Mbps to 50 Mbps per stream. Systems integrating with the Monitoring Package shall support sustained unicast video traffic within this range.
RTSP streams are accessible on the internal Monitoring Package network without authentication by default.
Resolution and frame rate are implementation defined and may vary by deployment.
6.5 Mechanical Interface to Host ROV
This section defines the mechanical integration boundary between the Monitoring Package and a Host ROV. The Monitoring Package mounting framework is developed using a constraint driven CAD workflow to accommodate host specific geometry. However, interoperability is maintained by holding a small set of reference features, orientation requirements, and keep clear volumes constant across integrations. A Host ROV integrator can fabricate a compatible adapter bracket by implementing the requirements in Sections 6.5.1 through 6.5.2 and verifying against the interface items summarized in Table 6-5-2.
6.5.1 Mounting Interface 
The Monitoring Package accommodates a variety of mounting geometries to a range of host ROV’s. The mounting interface is defined as a host-specific adapter arrangement that is generated and checked in CAD for each vehicle, while preserving a consistent set of alignment features and constraints on the Monitoring Package side. In practice, the Monitoring Package may be integrated as a single rigid assembly, or as a distributed set of attachment points that locate the CCB and external modules relative to the host frame, depending on the available structure, hydrodynamic constraints, and clearance limitations on the host vehicle.
To support this variability without ambiguity, the interface is defined by what the mount must accomplish rather than prescribing a single bracket geometry. During host integration, the mounting concept is selected and refined in CAD based on the host frame, clearances, and the alignment needs of the installed modules. Some hosts will use a fully fixed mount that locks the Monitoring Package in a single rigid pose, while others will incorporate a tilt axis to allow alignment to be dialed in during operations. In all cases, the adapter hardware shall locate the package from the Monitoring Package reference features, preserve the defined forward and up directions, and carry operational and handling loads through the primary structure rather than through sensor housings, penetrators, or connector bodies.
The controlling geometry for this interface is provided in the project CAD assembly “MoorSEA Monitoring Package.f3z” (attached to this report). This assembly is the reference for component locations, overall envelope, keep-clear volumes, and service access requirements. Host-specific integrations are developed by importing the Host ROV model, or a simplified host envelope, into this assembly and checking fit, alignment, access, and cable routing within the same coordinate system. For each host vehicle, the deliverable is the resulting verified CAD configuration, which serves as the reviewable definition of the mechanical interface even when the bracket and attachment layout vary between vehicles.

Glossary
ACOUSTIC TRANSCEIVER—A device that both emits and receives acoustic signals, used in underwater positioning systems such as USBL to measure range and bearing to subsea targets.
ALTERNATING CURRENT (AC)—Flow of electricity that constantly changes direction between positive and negative sides. Almost all power produced by electric utilities in the United States moves in current that shifts direction at a rate of 60 times per second.
ALTIMETER—A sensor that measures the distance between the ROV and the seafloor or another structure beneath it. Useful for maintaining safe standoff distances during inspection.
AZIMUTH–The horizontal angular measurement in degrees from a fixed reference direction, typically true north. In offshore positioning systems, azimuth is used to describe the direction from the surface transceiver to the ROV or subsea target.
COMPASS CALIBRATION—The process of adjusting a magnetic compass to compensate for local magnetic interference caused by nearby materials or electronics. Includes correction for hard and soft iron distortion.
DAUGHTER BOARD—A smaller circuit board that plugs into a backplane or motherboard, typically performing a specific function such as sensor input, power regulation, or data processing.
DEPTH SENSOR—A pressure based sensor that measures the ambient water pressure to determine the depth of the monitoring package or ROV below the surface.
DOPPLER VELOCITY LOG (DVL)—An acoustic sensor that measures the velocity of an underwater vehicle relative to the sea floor by emitting sound waves and analyzing the Doppler shift of the returned signals. DVLs are essential for precise underwater navigation.
DIRECT CURRENT (DC)—A charge of electricity that flows in one direction and is the type of power that comes from a battery. 
ELECTROMAGNETIC INTERFERENCE (EMI)—Unwanted noise or interference in an electrical path or circuit caused by an external electromagnetic field. EMI can degrade the performance of electronic devices and is mitigated through shielding and design practices.
ETHERNET—A family of networking technologies used for local area networks (LANs), enabling devices to communicate over a common network medium using standardized protocols.
FIELD OF VIEW (FOV)—The angular extent of the observable area captured by a sensor or camera. Measured in degrees, it defines the width of the image or sonar scan at a given distance.
GHOST GEAR—Abandoned, lost, or discarded fishing gear that remains in the marine environment. It can entangle marine life or become caught on offshore infrastructure.
GYROSCOPE (RATE GYRO)—A sensor that measures angular velocity. In subsea applications, it supports short term heading estimation and motion smoothing between compass updates.
HARD IRON DISTORTION—Magnetic interference caused by materials that produce a permanent magnetic field, such as ferrous metals with residual magnetism. Unlike soft iron distortion, which varies with orientation, hard magnetic distortion introduces a consistent directional bias in compass readings and must be corrected through calibration.
INTER-INTEGRATED CIRCUIT (I²C)—A synchronous, multi master, multi slave, packet switched, single ended, serial communication bus used for attaching low speed peripherals to processors and microcontrollers. It uses two lines: Serial Data Line (SDA) and Serial Clock Line (SCL).
INSPECTION CLASS ROV (iROV)—A category of remotely operated vehicles designed primarily for observation and inspection tasks. These ROVs are typically small, equipped with cameras and lights, and may have limited manipulator capabilities.
IP (Internet Protocol)—A set of rules that govern how data is sent and received across digital networks. IP enables communication between devices on local networks and the internet by assigning unique addresses and routing packets of information between them.
LIGHT WORK CLASS ROV (lwROV)—A type of remotely operated vehicle that is larger than inspection-class ROVs and capable of performing light intervention tasks. They are often equipped with manipulators and can operate at significant depths.
LUX—A unit of illuminance equal to one lumen per square meter. Used to specify the minimum light intensity required for visual sensing performance in low light environments.
MACHINE LEARNING—A computational method by which software systems improve their performance over time by learning patterns from data. Used here for automated entanglement detection from video input.
NEUTRAL BUOYANCY—A state in which the monitoring package displaces a volume of water equal to its own weight, resulting in no net force upward or downward. Essential for stable subsea positioning.
PARAMETRIC MODELING—A computer aided design (CAD) approach that uses parameters (e.g., dimensions, angles) to define object geometry. Enables rapid design adjustments and prototyping.
PRESSURE BOTTLE—A sealed housing designed to contain and protect sensitive electronics in high pressure underwater environments. Often used to house power and communication hardware in subsea systems.
REMOTELY OPERATED VEHICLE (ROV)—An unmanned, tethered underwater vehicle used for inspection, data collection, or intervention in subsea environments. Controlled from a surface vessel or platform.
RS-232 / RS-485—Standard serial communication protocols used to transmit data between embedded systems and peripherals. Often converted internally to Ethernet for compatibility.
SECONDARY ENTANGLEMENT—A condition in which marine debris, such as ghost gear, either already attached to marine animals or caught on mooring lines, increases the risk of further entanglement or injury.
SENSOR FUSION–The process of combining data from multiple sensors to produce a more accurate, reliable, or complete estimate than could be achieved from a single sensor alone. In subsea applications, sensor fusion may integrate compass and gyroscope data to improve heading stability.
SERIAL PERIPHERAL INTERFACE (SPI)—A synchronous serial communication interface used for short-distance communication, primarily in embedded systems. It operates in full duplex mode and uses separate lines for data transmission and reception.
SOFT IRON DISTORTION—Magnetic interference caused by materials that distort magnetic fields without retaining them (e.g., stainless steel structures). Must be compensated for in compass calibration.
SOFT START—A power management technique that gradually increases current to electrical components at startup to prevent sudden inrush currents that may trip protections or cause wear.
SONAR—A system that uses sound waves to detect and image underwater structures, debris, or marine life. Essential for navigation and inspection in low visibility environments.
STANDARDIZED INTERFACE—A defined set of mechanical, electrical, and communication specifications that enable compatibility between components or systems from different vendors or platforms.
STEREOSCOPIC IMAGING—A method of capturing and displaying visual information using two spatially separated cameras to simulate human binocular vision. In subsea applications, stereoscopic imaging enables depth perception by analyzing the disparity between the two images, allowing operators or automated systems to estimate distances to objects. This technique improves spatial awareness during inspection tasks and supports three-dimensional mapping and object recognition.
SUBSEA—Refers to equipment or operations conducted underwater, typically at significant ocean depths.
TCP (TRANSMISSION CONTROL PROTOCOL)—A reliable, connection oriented protocol used in the IP suite to ensure accurate and ordered delivery of data between devices. TCP is commonly used for telemetry and control communications where data integrity is critical.
TETHER MANAGEMENT—The active monitoring and control of the cable connecting the ROV to the surface. Important for preventing cable entanglement and minimizing drag.
USER DATAGRAM PROTOCOL (UDP)—A lightweight communication protocol used within the IP suite for sending short messages with minimal delay. UDP does not guarantee delivery, order, or data integrity, making it ideal for real time applications like video streaming.
ULTRA SHORT BASELINE ACOUSTIC POSITIONING SYSTEM (USBL)—An underwater acoustic positioning system that calculates the position of a target by measuring the range and bearing from a vessel-mounted transceiver to an acoustic transponder on the target. USBL systems are commonly used for tracking underwater vehicles and divers.
